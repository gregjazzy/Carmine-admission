The BFI at Jeannine Manuel: How It Changes Post-Graduation Options

Photo: École Jeannine Manuel

Ecole Jeannine Manuel January 2026 · updated November 2026 5 min read

The BFI comes up again and again in conversations among parents at École Jeannine Manuel. Since 2022, the French International Baccalaureate (BFI) has replaced the former international option of the baccalauréat (OIB), and many families have only a vague idea of what it changes. I have worked with students of the school up to their final year for more than ten years, and I support some of them through their university applications. Here is what the BFI really changes, country by country, and when the IB, the school's other track, is the better choice.

The BFI in practice

The BFI is a French baccalauréat with additional exams in the language of the section. At Jeannine Manuel, that is the American section, with Advanced Placement courses. Students follow the full French curriculum, core subjects, specialities and the grand oral, and sit two extra exams in English: cultural and linguistic studies, and knowledge of the world. The diploma carries the "Baccalauréat Français International" designation.

The workload is real: extra hours of literature and civilisation in English, an additional oral exam, and exams that count towards the final grade. A student who excels in them benefits; a student who neglects them sees their average drop.

BFI or IB: the school offers both

In the last two years of lycée, Jeannine Manuel offers two programmes, both taken at the school. The BFI is a French baccalauréat. The IB is an international diploma awarded by the International Baccalaureate Organization, taught mostly in English, in which the school keeps French as a compulsory subject. An IB student does not sit the French baccalauréat.

The choice also shapes the budget. In 2026-2027, BFI fees are €10,275 a year; in the IB track, the seconde year (Foundation year) costs €24,865, then the two diploma years €32,560 a year, plus a first-time registration fee of €1,650. For the wider trade-off between the two systems, see IB or French baccalaureate.

For American universities

American universities look at whether a student took the most demanding programme their school offers. The BFI in the American section, with its Advanced Placement courses, answers that question, provided the grades follow. The school prepares its students early: from troisième (age 14), they attend presentations on higher education and on the countries where they might study.

The BFI is not enough on its own: it has to be presented in the language of the universities you are targeting. Let's talk about your child's plans: I guide them through university choices and essays.

For British universities

In the UK, offers are conditional: each university sets, for the French baccalauréat, an overall average and subject grades to achieve. The BFI is a French baccalauréat and is read as such through UCAS. The exact requirements, including for English, should be checked course by course on each university's website.

For Switzerland

EPFL admits holders of the French baccalauréat on their grades, with requirements in mathematics and physics. The BFI is read there as a French baccalauréat. What counts first is the choice of specialities and the grades obtained; I go through the conditions in getting into EPFL from a French high school.

For French programmes

This is where the BFI keeps its clearest advantage over the IB. As a French baccalauréat, it goes through Parcoursup, the French admissions platform, without conversion: French-format reports, grades out of 20, national specialities. An IB file requires a reading that not every admissions panel makes with the same ease, particularly in the scientific classes préparatoires. For Sciences Po, the BFI is an asset: it shows bilingualism and an extra workload.

A diploma that keeps several doors open

The strength of the BFI is that it closes no route: the same file can aim at an American or British university, EPFL and a French prépa. It suits a student whose plans are not yet settled at the end of seconde.

It is not for everyone. If written French is fragile, if mathematics is only just adequate, if the student is already stretched by the standard programme, adding the BFI can pull down the overall average, and an international designation does not make up for average grades. The choice should rest on an honest assessment of the student, not on the appeal of the label.
