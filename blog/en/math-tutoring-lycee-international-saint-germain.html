Math Tutoring for Lycee International de Saint-Germain-en-Laye Students

Private Tutoring February 2026 10 min read

The Lycee International de Saint-Germain-en-Laye occupies a unique place in the French educational landscape. With fourteen national sections -- American, British, German, Spanish, Japanese, and many others -- it educates a student population of extraordinary diversity. But this diversity, which constitutes the school's cultural richness, creates a precise pedagogical challenge in mathematics: math classes are taught in French, for all students, regardless of their section. A student in the American or British section who thinks in English, who was trained in an Anglo-Saxon educational system, must master mathematics in their most rigorous form -- the French form -- in a language that is not always their dominant one. A generic tutor who does not understand this reality cannot help effectively.

Having worked with over 1,600 students, including many from the Lycee International de Saint-Germain, I understand this challenge from the inside. The issue is never a lack of ability. It is a gap between mathematical habits acquired in the student's original system and the demands of the French curriculum as taught at Saint-Germain.

What makes Saint-Germain's math program specific

The Lycee International de Saint-Germain-en-Laye follows the French national mathematics curriculum. There is no "lighter" version for students in international sections. A student in the American section follows exactly the same math program as a student in the French section. The same theorems, the same proofs, the same bac-style exercises, the same written assessments. The only difference is that this student must also manage the specific demands of their national section in parallel.

The BFI (Baccalaureat Francais International) reinforces this requirement. BFI mathematics exams are more rigorous than those of the standard baccalaureat. The international component of the diploma adds a significant workload -- section exams, "connaissance du monde" -- on top of an already dense mathematics program. Students at the Lycee International do not do less math than anyone else. They do as much, with more constraints around it.

The size of the school also plays a role. The Lycee International is a large institution with significant class sizes. Individualized mathematics support is structurally limited. A student who falls behind in Seconde will not necessarily have the opportunity to catch up within the school framework. Time passes, chapters pile up, and gaps solidify. This is precisely where the intervention of a specialized tutor makes the difference.

The specific difficulties of international section students

The central challenge for students in Anglo-Saxon sections -- American and British in particular -- is the methodological shock with French mathematics. This shock is the same one experienced by students arriving at Jeanine Manuel from abroad, but it manifests differently at Saint-Germain because the student is already settled in France, often for several years, and those around them assume the language issue is resolved. It is not.

In French mathematics, the core of an exercise is not finding the right answer. It is demonstrating why the answer is correct. A structured proof with hypotheses, theorems cited by name, rigorous logical chaining, and a formal conclusion. A student trained in the American system -- where the emphasis is on the result, where tests are often multiple-choice, where the graphing calculator is omnipresent -- must fundamentally reprogram how they do mathematics. It is not enough to understand the concepts. They must express them in a format that is culturally French.

Students in the German section face a different but equally real challenge. The German system teaches mathematics rigorously, but the writing conventions differ from the French format. Notation, presentation of reasoning, the way properties are cited -- all of this must be relearned in French conventions.

Students in the Spanish, Italian, or Portuguese sections generally fare better, as the mathematical traditions of Latin countries are closer to the French tradition. But even for them, the pace of the Lycee International and the demands of the BFI create areas of fragility, particularly in Terminale when workload peaks.

Another major challenge is managing bilingualism in a mathematical context. At Saint-Germain, students spend their days navigating between two languages. In mathematics, this navigation creates additional cognitive load. French mathematical vocabulary is dense and specific. A student who must mentally translate "remainder" to "reste," "derivative" to "derivee," "proof by contradiction" to "demonstration par l'absurde" loses a few seconds each time which, over a two-hour exam, translate into lost points.

Why generic tutoring fails at Saint-Germain

The tutoring market in the Yvelines is not short of math tutors. But virtually all of them work with the standard curriculum, for standard students, in standard schools. The Lycee International de Saint-Germain is not a standard school. A tutor who does not understand the specifics of the student population -- the bilingualism, the methodological gap, the dual demands of the BFI -- will not correctly diagnose the student's difficulties and therefore will not address them effectively.

A concrete example: a student in the American section making errors in analytic geometry. A generic tutor will redo the exercises, re-explain the formulas, assign practice. But the real problem, in most cases, is not that the student does not understand analytic geometry. It is that they do not know how to write a proof in analytic geometry in the French format. They arrive at the correct result but lose half the marks because their writing does not meet the expected conventions. A tutor who knows Saint-Germain recognizes immediately that it is the format, not the substance, that is the problem, and addresses it directly.

Physics and chemistry: the same challenge

Everything said about mathematics applies with equal force to physics and chemistry. The physics-chemistry program at the Lycee International follows the French curriculum, with its specific requirements for written reasoning, dimensional analysis, equation setup, and experimental rigor. A student from the British section who learned physics by "solving for x" without ever writing a complete physics argument will be penalized on assessments -- not because they do not understand the physics, but because they do not express it in the expected format.

Chemistry raises specific vocabulary and convention issues. French organic nomenclature, redox equation writing, the presentation of reaction progress tables -- all technical skills that cannot be improvised and require targeted instruction.
