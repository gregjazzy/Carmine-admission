Collège Sévigné: a secular, demanding school without competition

Collège Sévigné, rue Pierre-Nicole, Paris 5th. Photo LPLT, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Among the leading Paris schools, Sévigné is the one people talk about least and know worst. It has neither the fame of the Alsacienne nor the international image of Jeannine Manuel. Yet its identity is sharper than most, and that identity decides whether a child will be happy there.

A history that explains everything

Sévigné opened in 1880 with a pioneering ambition: to give girls, including those from the Jewish and Protestant minorities, access to academic excellence, in a spirit of neutrality. It was the first lycée for girls in France. The school became co-educational in the early 1970s, entered into contract with the state in 1980, and became bilingual from 2016. It remains a secular non-profit association, with a motto borrowed from Pascal: "Let us work at thinking well".

Demanding, but without rankings

Sévigné's formula, "excellence without competition", comes from Mathilde Salomon, who led the school from 1883. It means real academic demands, a strong place for the humanities, classical languages and general culture, philosophy from the last year of nursery, and an explicit refusal to set students against one another. A student who struggles is neither dropped nor eased out: that is a rule of the school.

Small, by choice

Around 300 primary pupils, 370 collège students and 390 lycée students: three classes per year group in collège, four in lycée. It is a deliberate size, which allows everyone to be known and followed. It has a counterpart: fewer rare options and a tighter school life than in the very large schools. The school also has a higher-education department that has prepared candidates for teaching competitions for over a century, giving it close links with universities and research in education.

For which child

I have students at Sévigné, as at the other leading Paris schools, and every year I support around fifteen families with their admissions. Sévigné suits remarkably well the curious child who loves reading and discussing, and who gives their best when they feel trusted rather than compared. It suits less the child who needs the spur of rankings, or the family looking above all for a label. That diagnosis, on the real child, is what I make with every family before choosing schools.

A school on the move

The 2026-2030 school plan provides for a trilingual French International Baccalaureate, an "integrated sciences" module for lycée students aiming at selective science programmes, a fablab in collège, and a target of 10% of lycée students on scholarships. Sévigné sees itself as a "pathfinder": it experiments a great deal, and stands by it.
