Math Tutoring for La Tour Students

Private Tutoring February 2026 10 min read

La Tour does not compromise on academic standards. Located in Paris's 16th arrondissement, this private school has built a solid reputation among the city's most demanding families, and that reputation rests on a concrete reality: a mathematics and science program that pushes students to their highest level, with expectations that tolerate neither approximation nor complacency. A student who begins to fall behind in math at La Tour quickly finds themselves in a critical situation, because the pace never slows and gaps multiply at a speed that parents regularly underestimate. A generic tutor who knows the standard French curriculum but not the specifics of La Tour cannot meet this challenge.

Drawing on experience with over 1,600 students, including a significant number from La Tour and comparable institutions in the 16th arrondissement, I understand precisely the nature of this challenge. It is not a problem of intellectual capacity. It is a problem of alignment between the student's pace and the expectations of an institution that does not wait.

What distinguishes La Tour's math program

La Tour follows the French national mathematics curriculum but applies it with a level of rigor that places it among the highest-performing lycees in Paris. The mathematics faculty is composed of teachers who understand the expectations of the most selective post-bac programs and calibrate their instruction to prepare students for them. Written assessments are long, complex, and graded with a rigor that goes beyond what is found in the majority of French schools.

In the mathematics specialization, the program is dense and demanding. Proofs must be written with impeccable formalism. Open-ended "problem" exercises, where students must draw on knowledge from multiple chapters to construct an original argument, are frequent. Grading prioritizes the quality of reasoning over the correctness of the final answer: a correct calculation that is poorly justified receives only a fraction of the marks, while a well-structured argument with a minor computational error is valued much more highly.

This level of rigor has a direct consequence: performance gaps between students widen rapidly. The strongest students progress at a sustained pace and naturally prepare for the best post-bac programs. Those who struggle accumulate deficits, lose confidence, and see their results drop -- often dramatically -- between Seconde and Premiere. This is a pattern we observe repeatedly, and one that generic tutoring is structurally incapable of breaking.

The typical struggles of La Tour students

The profile of a La Tour student struggling in math is rarely that of a disengaged or lazy student. It is most often a serious, conscientious student who works hard but works poorly -- or rather, works in ways that are misaligned with what the school expects. Several mechanisms explain this mismatch.

The first is the confusion between understanding and mastery. A student who understands the lesson often believes, incorrectly, that they have mastered it. Understanding a theorem in class and being able to apply it under pressure in a two-hour written assessment are two fundamentally different things. At La Tour, assessments are designed to test mastery, not surface-level understanding. The student who has merely understood, without training intensively, discovers the difference in their grades.

The second is inadequate mathematical writing. Many La Tour students lose marks not because they cannot solve the exercises, but because they write them up poorly. French mathematical writing is an exercise in itself: every step must be justified, every theorem cited by name, every implication made explicit. A student who "skips steps" or who "sees" the solution without writing it out formally loses marks systematically. And at La Tour, where grading is demanding, these marks lost on writing make the difference between a 14 and a 10.

The third is time management. La Tour's written assessments are long and demanding. A student who masters the content but cannot manage their time -- who spends too long on a difficult exercise at the expense of more accessible ones -- achieves results below their actual level. Time management is a skill in itself that must be learned and practiced, but that most generic tutors do not teach.

The trap of generic tutoring

The 16th arrondissement is a saturated market for academic tutoring. Options abound: prepa students, graduates of grandes ecoles, retired teachers, online platforms. The problem is not availability. It is relevance.

A generic tutor re-explains the lesson. They redo textbook exercises. They assign additional practice. This is honest work, but it misses the target for a La Tour student. Because this student's problem is generally not that they do not understand the lesson. It is that they do not know how to apply it at the level of rigor that La Tour expects. And to work at that level, the tutor must know that level. They must know what La Tour's teachers expect in assessments, how they grade, what types of exercises they favor, and what methodological errors they penalize most severely.

A tutor who does not know La Tour works in the dark. They may be excellent at mathematics and poor at pedagogy adapted to this institution. The result is an investment of time and money that does not produce the expected results.

Physics and chemistry: the same battle

The physics-chemistry program at La Tour mirrors the mathematics program: demanding, structured, and rigorously assessed. Students targeting scientific preparatory classes must excel in both subjects, and difficulties in physics-chemistry follow the same patterns as in mathematics.

Setting up equations for physics problems is a frequent blocking point. Many students understand physical phenomena but are unable to translate them into workable equations. They can describe qualitatively what is happening, but they cannot formalize their understanding. La Tour expects this formalization, and assessments are designed to test it.

Chemistry presents specific challenges of precision and rigor. Concentration calculations, chemical equilibria, and redox reactions demand attention to detail and mastery of units that cannot be improvised. A specialized tutor knows where the fragility points lie and how to strengthen them.
