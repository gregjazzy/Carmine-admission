The Math Gap at Jeannine Manuel: How to Bridge It

This is the most common weakness in applications from abroad: underestimating the gap between the mathematics learnt elsewhere and the mathematics École Jeannine Manuel expects. Every year, students who shine in their own system find themselves struggling with French mathematics. Not for lack of ability: they learnt to do mathematics differently. I have taught mathematics to students of the school for more than ten years, and closing this gap is what I do with children preparing their admission.

The French way: prove, not just calculate

In France, mathematics is not only about finding the right answer: you have to prove it is right, in a written argument, step by step, leaving nothing implicit. A student in seconde (age 15) learns to reason by contradiction, to write a proof with its hypotheses and conclusion, to use standard identities to factorise, and to study how a function varies while justifying each step.

This demand for written reasoning sits at the heart of French teaching, and it is where students trained in other systems stumble.

The American system: methods and results

The American system mostly teaches methods: in Algebra II or Pre-Calculus, students solve equations and systems and work with functions, and many assessments focus on the final answer. They are rarely asked to prove in writing why a method works. A student with an A in Pre-Calculus may never have written a French-style algebraic proof, while understanding the concepts perfectly well.

The IB: conceptual, but with a calculator

The IB Middle Years Programme and Diploma Programme take a more conceptual approach: understanding, links between topics, real-world applications. It is good teaching, with two gaps compared with French expectations. The graphing calculator is everywhere, whereas in France students must be able to calculate and manipulate expressions by hand. And written work is less formal: a few lines of working and a boxed answer, where a French marker expects the justifications and the properties used.

The British system: solid techniques

GCSEs and A-Levels produce students who calculate efficiently and handle standard problems well. Proof, however, plays a limited role before the most advanced levels. A Year 11 student knows their theorems and how to apply them, but has rarely written out a full geometric proof starting "Let ABC be a triangle such that…".

Where the gaps are

Writing proofs

A French proof follows a precise format: hypotheses, properties cited, chained deductions, conclusion, with expected wording ("by Thales' theorem", "but", "therefore"). A student who has never practised it cannot improvise it.

Algebraic manipulation

In troisième and seconde (ages 14 and 15), standard identities are used to factorise: seeing that 4x² − 12x + 9 is the square of (2x − 3) is far from obvious for a student who only knows them as formulas to apply.

Studying functions

From seconde, students draw up and justify a table of variations; later in lycée come derivatives, limits and the full study of a function. A student who has always plotted functions on a calculator has never built that reasoning.

Justified geometry

In French middle school, geometry asks students to justify every step of a construction or a proof. It is a much less prominent tradition in English-speaking systems.

Closing the gap in three to six months

Here is how I work with a student coming from another system.

The first two months: foundations

A precise assessment of the gaps against the French curriculum for the target year, then French mathematical writing: vocabulary, notation, the structure of a proof. Calculator-free algebra, and the conventions of French geometry.

The next two months: consolidation

Progressive exercises from French textbooks, written proofs in geometry and algebra, studies of functions, timed calculator-free exercises, marked against French criteria.

The last two months: practice

Tests built to the school's level, marked in detail on the writing as much as on the answers, and targeted work on the remaining weaknesses. The goal is a script that "reads" French: structured, justified, clear.

With less than three months, preparation is still possible, but more intensive, and focused on the gaps that pay off most.

Three to six months, provided you start early. Tell me their year and school system and I will suggest a lesson schedule built around their test date.

The trap of good grades

The most insidious danger is good grades. A student scoring 95% at their international school, their parents and the student themselves assume no maths preparation is needed. That figure reflects mastery of their own school's programme, in their own school's format; it says nothing about their ability to succeed at mathematics the French way. It is the most common gap, and the most avoidable.
