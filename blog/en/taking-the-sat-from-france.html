Taking the SAT from a French lycée

Standardised tests October 2026 12 min read

Families in the French system approach the SAT as a mathematics exam. It is the most expensive mistake in an American application, and it costs hundreds of points. The SAT is not a test of level. It is a test of speed, reading and nerve, built on a mathematics syllabus that stops at the level of première, the penultimate year of the French lycée (upper secondary school).

This article sets out what the test contains, what it costs, when to sit it from France, and which score serves which purpose. Everything that follows comes from the College Board and from the universities themselves.

The SAT has changed: it is digital, and it adapts

The test is taken on a computer, in the Bluebook app, in two hours and fourteen minutes. Only two sections remain: Reading and Writing, then Math, separated by a ten-minute break.

This mechanism has a consequence many people overlook: two students with the same number of correct answers may not receive the same score, because the difficulty of the questions answered correctly feeds into the calculation. Rushing the first module shuts off access to the harder second module, and therefore to the top of the scale.

Another change: a calculator is allowed throughout the Math section. The no-calculator part has disappeared. A graphing calculator is built into the app and can be used at any time, and students may also bring their own, provided it is not a model with a computer algebra system.

What the test covers, and how far it goes

The Math section is split into four domains, in published proportions.

It needs saying plainly: for a student in première or terminale (the final year of lycée) who has taken the mathematics spécialité, the in-depth subject option of the French baccalauréat, there is nothing on this list they have not already seen. What will surprise them is American-style statistics, margin of error, observational studies versus experiments, inference, which the French syllabus does not establish.

The Reading and Writing section is also split into four domains: Craft and Structure for about twenty-eight per cent, Information and Ideas for twenty-six, Standard English Conventions for twenty-six, and Expression of Ideas for twenty.

What a French-system student misses, and it is not what you think

The Math section poses no problem of level for a student in première or terminale who has taken the spécialité. The content covered stops well short of what France teaches: no integrals, no sequences, no complex numbers, no conditional probability in the French sense. A good student recognises everything they see.

What brings them down is pace and wording. Each question allows barely more than a minute. The questions are in English, often long, and half the work consists of translating a concrete situation into an equation before any calculation begins. A French student used to terse questions and three-part exercises loses time on the reading and fails to reach the end of the section. They do not lose points because they cannot do the maths. They lose them because they have never been trained to go fast on easy material.

The real gap lies elsewhere, in the Reading and Writing section. That is where a French-system student hits a ceiling, and that is where the difference between a respectable score and a score that counts is decided. Three reasons:

  • Vocabulary in context. The questions ask which word an author chose and why. The expected register is that of serious American journalism and academic writing. English as taught in French schools, even in the international sections, does not establish it.
  • Writing conventions. Punctuation, agreement, sentence structure, logical transitions. These are precise rules, entirely learnable, and nobody teaches them in English lessons in France because they belong to the syllabus for native speakers.
  • Rhetorical synthesis. You are given a set of loose notes and must choose the sentence that achieves a specific goal. It is an exercise in reasoning, not language, and it can be practised.

The practical consequence: a well-prepared French-system student almost always arrives with an unbalanced score, very strong in Math, average in Reading. Admissions committees see it immediately, and an unbalanced profile tells a story. It is that balance you need to work on, not the total.

The dates, and the real problem: seats

The College Board publishes eight test dates for 2026-2027, identical in France and in the United States. Registration closes about two weeks before, and scores are released about thirteen days after the test.

The real obstacle in France is not the date. It is the seat.

As of 25 September 2026, the official test centre search tool shows eight centres in France for the November session, three of them already full, and eleven for December, three of them full. The Paris centres fill first, while Bordeaux, Marseille, Rennes, Clermont-Ferrand and the South still have seats. Registering early is not a precaution. It is the main constraint of the calendar.

What score to aim for, and how to read the numbers

The total score runs from 400 to 1600, in steps of ten, as the sum of two sections each scored from 200 to 800. For the class of 2025, out of just over two million test takers, the average was 1029: 521 in Reading and Writing and 508 in Math. Seven per cent of test takers score above 1400.

Before looking at the ranges for the leading universities, one point of method that changes everything: none of them publishes the scores of its admitted students. All of them publish those of their enrolled students, and only of those who chose to submit a score. When a university is test-optional and only one enrolled student in four submits a score, its range describes nothing more than a self-selected minority. That is the column to look at first.

Now read the same chart another way. Georgetown shows the lowest range on the list, but also the highest submission rate, nearly eight enrolled students in ten. NYU shows a high range with fewer than one enrolled student in four submitting. Georgetown's range therefore describes a real class; NYU's describes a shop window.

The second thing to know, and it is moving fast: the test is becoming compulsory again. Harvard, Yale, MIT, Stanford and Georgetown require it for 2027 entry. Princeton and Columbia are still test-optional for 2027 but have announced a return to requiring it for the following intake. A student currently in première no longer really has a choice.

One figure to settle the matter: since Harvard made the test compulsory, its submission rate has risen from about half to nearly eight in ten, and the bottom of its Reading and Writing range has fallen from 740 to 720. The requirement widens the pool instead of narrowing it.

What it costs

Sixty-eight dollars to register, plus a forty-three dollar international fee from France, making one hundred and eleven dollars. Late registration adds thirty-eight dollars, a change of test centre thirty-four, and a cancellation thirty-four as well. Each additional score report costs fifteen dollars.

These fees apply until December 2026. The College Board publishes no fee for changing the test date, only for changing the test centre.

Sending scores without shooting yourself in the foot

Four score sends are free with each registration, to be designated when you register or within nine days after the test. After that, fifteen dollars each.

The option called Score Choice lets you choose, test date by test date, what you send. Two caveats, published by the College Board: it does not apply to the four free sends designated at registration, and some universities require all results. It is also impossible to mix sections, that is, to send the Math score from one date and the Reading and Writing score from another.

This is where the word everyone misuses comes in. The superscore is not a College Board service. It is a practice of certain universities, which themselves rebuild the best total from sections taken on different dates. You send them everything and they do the sorting. Check each university's policy before deciding what to send.

Preparing without paying

The College Board publishes, free of charge, enough to cover most of the work. The Bluebook app contains full-length practice tests, timed, scored and genuinely adaptive, meaning they reproduce the mechanism of test day. Eight additional practice tests exist in paper format, numbered four to eleven, but they are not adaptive and are recommended only for students sitting the test on paper because of accommodations.

On top of this come a bank of thousands of real questions that can be filtered by domain and difficulty, a personalised practice tool based on your results, and the official partnership with Khan Academy, also free.

The College Board does not publish how many full-length tests Bluebook contains. It states only that they can be retaken without limit.

When to start, really

The short answer: the end of seconde (the first year of lycée) for a first attempt in première, with a second in terminale if needed. The long answer comes down to one observation from the field.

Families who start at the beginning of terminale face two problems at once. They prepare for the SAT while writing the essays, building the university list and handling the continuous assessment that counts towards the baccalauréat. The SAT is the first thing to give way, and it gives way at the worst moment, because Early Decision and Early Action deadlines fall at the beginning of November.

A student who sits the test for the first time in the spring of première has a score in hand, knows where the gaps are, and can decide with full knowledge. It is not a question of extra work. It is a question of order.

And for the United Kingdom or Europe?

A frequent question, and a clear answer for the United Kingdom: with a French baccalauréat, none of the leading British universities requires the SAT.

Cambridge asks for 17 out of 20 in the baccalauréat, with the "mention très bien" (highest honours), and does not mention the SAT in its page for France. It even states that if you have taken the SAT alongside a qualification that is sufficient on its own, such as the baccalauréat, your results will not be considered. Imperial College asks for 15 out of 20 in the baccalauréat and mentions the SAT only for the American route. The London School of Economics asks for a "mention très bien" or "mention bien" (high honours) depending on the programme, without a word about the SAT. The SAT appears, at Oxford as elsewhere, only for applicants from an American high school, alongside AP exams.

For continental Europe, the answer depends on each institution and has to be checked one by one. Bocconi, for example, accepts the SAT in place of its own test, with published thresholds. Others ignore it completely. Do not generalise.
