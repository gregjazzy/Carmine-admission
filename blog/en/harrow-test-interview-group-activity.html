The Harrow Test: interview, online assessment and group activity

The Harrow Test is the second and decisive round of 13+ admission. A boy reaches it only after his Pre-Test and his school reference have cleared the first filter, and after he has sent a handwritten CV. It takes place at the School in one of ten sessions between early January and mid-February of Year 6, and it has three parts. Harrow publishes no past papers for it, which is precisely why families misjudge it.

Part one: twenty minutes with a House Master

Every applicant is interviewed by a House Master, who will be responsible, day and night, for some seventy boys in a boarding House. That tells you what the conversation is for. A House Master is asking himself a practical question: would I want this boy in my House for five years? Is he curious, does he engage with an adult, can he talk about what he does and why, will he cope away from home, will he add something to the life of the House?

The handwritten CV usually feeds the conversation. A boy who has listed chess, the cello and a Saturday rugby club will be asked about them, and the difference between "I like chess" and an account of the last game he lost and what he learned from it is the difference the interview is designed to find. Ten-year-olds are not expected to be polished. They are expected to be themselves, in full sentences, with some spark.

Part two: one hour of computerised English and mathematics

An on-screen assessment of about an hour covering English and mathematics, with non-verbal reasoning also mentioned among its components. It is the academic check that confirms, under Harrow's own conditions, what the Pre-Test suggested. Boys who have done all their practice on paper sometimes lose time simply navigating the screen; boys used to timed online work do not.

Harrow's own advice for preparation points families toward the kind of online practice tools many prep schools already use for 11+ work. The useful part is not the brand of platform but the habit: regular, timed, mixed practice in comprehension, arithmetic and problem solving, with mistakes reviewed rather than simply repeated.

Part three: twenty minutes of group activity

Applicants work together on a task in a classroom while staff observe. The format is short and the lesson is clear: Harrow wants to see how a boy behaves among other boys. Does he listen before speaking, does he build on someone else's idea, does he help the group finish rather than win the argument, does he include the quiet boy in the corner? A dominant boy who talks over everyone does himself no favours; neither does one who says nothing for twenty minutes.

What decides between strong candidates

By the time of the Harrow Test, every boy in the room has a good Pre-Test and a good reference. The academic hour therefore separates them less than parents expect, and the interview and group work more. That is the moment where preparation of a different kind pays: not more mathematics, but a boy who has practised talking to an unfamiliar adult, who can explain his interests with examples, and who knows how to work in a group of strangers.

Boys from schools abroad, or from systems where pupils rarely speak at length in class, are often underestimated here. A boy schooled in French or Spanish may answer in short, correct sentences that sound flat in English. Practice in conversational English about himself changes that within a few weeks.

What happens next

Results come in late April or early May of Year 6: an offer in a named House (A1), a guaranteed place with the House to follow (A2), the waiting list (B), or no offer (C). Boys with an A1 meet their House Master again, one to one, from the summer onward.

How I prepare a boy for the Harrow Test

I run full mock interviews in the format of a House Master conversation, built on the boy's own CV, and we review them together until he is relaxed and specific. For the assessment, timed on-screen practice in English and mathematics. For the group activity, short collaborative tasks with feedback on listening and leadership. The aim is a boy who arrives on the Hill knowing what to expect, and shows who he is.
