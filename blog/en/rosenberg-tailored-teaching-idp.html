Institut auf dem Rosenberg: ability-based teaching, tailored timetables and the IDP

Institut auf dem Rosenberg, St. Gallen. Photo Rene Nueesch, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0

Boarding schools November 2026 5 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

At Rosenberg, a thirteen-year-old can take maths with sixteen-year-olds and German with beginners. That is the principle of the St. Gallen school: teaching organised by ability rather than age, with a timetable unique to each student. I have helped students win places at Rosenberg and Aiglon, and fifteen at Le Rosey, and this approach is what sets Rosenberg apart, both as an opportunity and as a demand.

Ability before age

Rosenberg places each student according to their ability in each subject. In middle school, core subjects are taught at four levels; in high school, close to 500 courses allow almost limitless combinations, and every student receives a unique timetable. A child far ahead in maths does not wait for the others; a child arriving from another system catches up where needed, without being held back elsewhere. No two students follow the same week.

The individual development plan

This tailoring is held together by the IDP, the individual development plan, built for each student around their goals, strengths and ambitions. Guidance counsellors and academic mentors monitor progress and give regular feedback. Parents are part of the team, sharing their vision for their child's education with the school's specialists. The plan also prepares students for language certificates from A1 to C2 (Cambridge, Goethe, DELF), the SAT and IELTS, and an app lets them follow timetables, grades and communication with the school day to day.

What this system changes for a child

For a student with an uneven profile, brilliant in one subject and fragile in another, it is often a liberation. They are no longer a "good student" or an "average student": they move at their own pace in each subject. For a passionate student, it means the chance to go very far very early, up to university-level courses.

But the system also asks a lot. A tailored timetable means knowing how to choose, and choosing well. The IDP encourages students to reflect on their goals and take ownership of their education; a child who waits for others to decide gets much less out of it. In practice, the families who do best are those who see the plan as a shared project: they stay involved, read the feedback from mentors, and help their child make choices rather than making the choices for them.

Does your child have a very uneven profile? Describe them to me: that is often where Rosenberg makes the most sense.

What it means for admission

Because the school places students by ability, the entrance exam also decides where a child will start in each subject. Well prepared, they enter where they deserve to be; poorly prepared, they start below their true level. And because the school rests on choice, the interview looks for whether the child can already say what they love and where they want to go.

That is also why the personal statement matters so much. A child who has thought about what they want to study, and why, gives the admissions team something to build an individual plan around from the first day. The statement can take any form, a video, a collage, a presentation: so many chances to show how the child thinks, what drives them, and what they will do with the freedom the school offers. Families who treat it as a formality waste one of the most valuable pieces of the file.
