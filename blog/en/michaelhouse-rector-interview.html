The interview with the Rector

At most schools, a boy applying at 11 meets an admissions officer, if he meets anyone at all. At Michaelhouse, he meets the Rector, the head of the school, or one of the Deputy Rectors. The interview takes place during Grade 6, on a visit to Balgowan with his parents that also includes a tour of the school. For boys entering in later years, the same personal interview applies.

Where it sits in the file

Michaelhouse admits on four things: previous academic performance, recent school reports, a confidential recommendation from the boy's current Head, and this interview. Three of the four are written by others. The interview is the only part where the boy speaks for himself, which is why families are right to take it seriously, and wrong to turn it into an exam.

Five questions families ask

Do the parents attend? The visit is made by the boy and his parents together. How the conversation itself is organised is for the school to set; parents should follow its lead on the day.

What will he be asked? There is no list of questions to learn, and no right answers. A boy should expect to talk about his school, what he enjoys, what he finds hard, what he does outside lessons, and why Michaelhouse.

Does a shy boy lose out? No, as long as he says real things. A short, honest answer about something he cares about is worth more than a long rehearsed one.

Does he need to know the school's history? He should know why he wants to come. Knowing that every boy boards, that there are ten Houses and that the school expects boys to take part in sport and culture is enough to talk about it sincerely.

Can a weak interview be offset? The decision rests on the whole file. But a boy who does not want to come, and lets it show, gives the Rector a reason that is hard to ignore.

What helps, in practice

  • Real material. Two or three things he does and cares about, sport, music, reading, a project, which he can describe in detail.
  • Practice with strangers. A boy used to talking only to family and teachers benefits from a few conversations with adults he does not know.
  • Honesty about boarding. Five years of full boarding is a large step. A boy who has thought about it, and perhaps already spent Weekend@Michaelhouse on campus, speaks about it convincingly.

Is your son comfortable talking about himself? Let me hear him.

What does not help

Scripts learned by heart, answers written by parents, and long lists of achievements. Michaelhouse's founder wanted to form "men of understanding, thought and culture"; a boy who thinks aloud, even hesitantly, is closer to that than one who recites.

How I prepare a boy for it

With a boy aiming at Michaelhouse, I work through conversations, not scripts: finding the subjects that bring him to life, practising with someone new, and helping him say plainly why he wants to come.
