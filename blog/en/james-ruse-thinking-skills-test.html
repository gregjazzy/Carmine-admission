The Thinking Skills Test

The third section of the Selective High School Placement Test is Thinking Skills: 40 multiple-choice questions, four options each, in 40 minutes. It counts for a quarter of the result. It assesses general critical thinking and problem solving, and no previous knowledge is required.

Of the four sections, this is the one that looks least like anything children do at school, which is precisely why familiarity matters. I work with children on it every year. Here is how to approach it.

A minute per question

Forty questions in forty minutes leaves one minute each, the tightest pace of the test. The questions come in a range of types, all visible in the official practice tests. Broadly, they ask a child to reason with the information given on the screen, critically and to a solution. Since nothing has to be known in advance, the whole difficulty is in thinking clearly and quickly.

The pace changes the approach. A child cannot afford to reread a long question three times. Reading once, carefully, and identifying what is actually being asked before looking at the options, saves more time than any shortcut.

Why familiarity matters here

No special knowledge is needed for Thinking Skills. But meeting a question format for the very first time under a one-minute clock is a disadvantage that has nothing to do with ability. The free online practice tests exist to remove it: they reproduce the software and every question type, show which answers were wrong, and let the child review them. Working through them, and talking about why an answer is right, is the preparation the Department itself provides.

What does not help is memorising tricks for particular question types. The test is revised, versions differ, and a child who has learned to think through a problem adapts where a child who has learned a trick does not.

Habits that carry over

HabitWhat it prevents
Reading the question to the end before answeringAnswering the question one expected rather than the one asked
Writing down conditions on the A4 sheetLosing track in multi-step problems
Testing each option against the informationChoosing an option that sounds right but is not supported
Moving on after a fair attemptRunning out of time with easier questions left

These habits are not specific to the test. They serve children in mathematics, in reading and in discussion, which is a good reason to build them patiently rather than cram them.
