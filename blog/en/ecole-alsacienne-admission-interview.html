École alsacienne: the admission interview, ten minutes without the parents

The École alsacienne, Paris 6th. Photo Celette, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Paris schools November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

At most of the leading Paris schools, the admission interview is a family affair: the parents present their plans, the child fills in. At the École alsacienne, it is the other way round. From CE1 (age 7), the school no longer sees the parents, except in particular situations, and says so plainly: it recruits students, not their parents. The child goes in alone, facing one of the school's teachers, for about ten minutes.

It is the step families dread most, because on the day there is nothing they can do. It is also the one where I see the most excellent children lose a place they deserved.

An interview that comes after the sift

The interview is only offered to applicants whose file has been shortlisted and, for entry into CM1 and sixième, who have sat the French and maths test. When the child sits down in front of the teacher, their academic level is therefore already known. The school says it: the interview is there to take the student's personality and motivation into account. It is not an oral exam, it is a meeting, and that is exactly why it is so hard to prepare for.

Ten minutes that can be prepared

I prepare your child for the École alsacienne interview without making them recite anything: they learn to talk about themselves, what they love and what they think, in their own words and with confidence. My aim: that they walk into the room alone, and walk out having shown who they are.

What the teacher is really looking for

The École alsacienne sums up its project in three words: creativity, cooperation, confidence. It says it favours reflection over repetition, and rejects competition and elitism. On the website, these words look like those of every school. In the room, they become very concrete.

What I see, following students at the school and children applying to it, is that an Alsacienne teacher is not impressed by a child who has read everything, visited everything and done every activity. They pay attention to a child who thinks: who is surprised, who hesitates and then puts forward an idea, who talks about a book or a passion with real curiosity rather than listing their activities. A child reciting answers prepared by their parents is spotted within a minute, and it is the surest way to lose the place.

Is your child at ease talking about themselves to an adult they do not know? Tell me about them: I will tell you how to prepare them without moulding them.

The trap of parental preparation

Families' reflex is understandable: rehearse what the child will say, suggest the right words, the right books, the right passions. At the École alsacienne, it backfires. The school chose not to see the parents precisely in order to see the child as they are. Anything that smells of adult preparation turns against them.

Preparing a child well for this interview is the opposite: getting them used to talking to an unknown adult, developing an idea instead of answering in one word, saying they do not know and thinking out loud, talking about what they really love. That cannot be learned the night before.

What I do for applicants

I have students at the École alsacienne, as at the other leading Paris schools, and every year I support around fifteen families with their admissions. For the interview, I work with your child on what cannot be improvised:

  • practice sessions with an adult they do not know, in the interview's format, in person or by video;
  • how they talk about themselves, their reading and what drives them, in their own words;
  • the art of thinking out loud: developing an idea, accepting not knowing, responding to an unexpected question.

And because the interview is only one step, I also prepare the French and maths test, follow the application and the calendar with you, and build the list of other schools to aim for.

In nursery and CP: the parents, this time

For the youngest children, it is the family the school meets. In its own words, it is looking for partner parents whose commitment to the school's project is clear. The interview is less about the child than about the fit between what the family expects from a school and what the Alsacienne offers: a secular, caring education that rejects competition. Parents who come looking for a springboard to academic excellence, without embracing this philosophy, often show it without realising.

For families who do not live in Paris

The school allows the interview to take place by video call for students who do not live in Paris. The exercise is even more demanding: holding ten minutes in front of a screen, without the contact of a real meeting, requires an ease few children have naturally. It is a format that needs its own preparation.
