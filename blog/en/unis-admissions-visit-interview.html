The UNIS admissions visit: observation, group activity or interview

United Nations International School, on the East River, Manhattan. Photo Transpoman, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

New York independent schools November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

Every UNIS applicant comes to the East River campus at least once before a decision is made, and for most it is the only time the school meets them in person. UNIS says plainly why: it commits to meeting each child to make sure they will thrive there. What that meeting looks like depends entirely on the child's age, and families who prepare for the wrong format waste their effort.

Scene one: Pre-K to 4th grade, a morning in class

The youngest applicants come in person and take part in classroom activities alongside other children, while UNIS staff observe how they interact. There is no test and no question-and-answer session. Parents, meanwhile, join a live discussion with teachers; there is no parent interview anywhere in the process.

Observers at this age are not looking for reading or counting. They watch how a child enters a group, follows a shared instruction, copes with waiting and sharing, and finds a way to communicate. A child who is quiet for twenty minutes and then joins in is not penalised; a child who cannot let go of a parent's hand at the door, or who takes over every activity, gives the observers more to think about.

Scene two: 5th and 6th grade, the group activities

Around 45 minutes of activities with other applicants. The format tells you what matters: UNIS wants to see a ten- or eleven-year-old work with strangers. Listening while another child explains, adding to an idea rather than replacing it, accepting a role in a team, staying engaged when the task is not the one they would have chosen. Being the cleverest voice in the room is less useful than being the one others are glad to work with.

Children who have only ever worked alone at a desk, or who come from very teacher-led classrooms, can find this unexpectedly hard. They are not less able; they have simply never been asked to collaborate in front of observers.

Scene three: 7th grade and up, thirty minutes of conversation

From 7th grade, and for every Tutorial House applicant, the meeting is an individual conversation of about 30 minutes with UNIS staff. It is not an oral exam, but it is the moment where UNIS judges whether the student is ready for a school that expects independence and self-advocacy.

The questions that come up in such conversations are rarely surprising: what the student enjoys at school and why, a book or project they remember, something that went wrong and how they handled it, what they do outside class, why UNIS. What separates candidates is not the content of the answers but their quality: concrete examples, a sense of the student's own voice, and the ability to keep a conversation going rather than waiting for the next question.

Ending with a real question about the school, one that shows the student has looked at what UNIS actually offers, is worth more than any rehearsed closing line.

Is your child more at ease in a group or one to one? Let's talk about it.

The tour, and what to ask

For older applicants, the meeting is usually booked together with a 45-minute tour of the campus. Use it. Ask how new students are integrated mid-way through a division, how language levels are set for a child arriving with no French or Spanish, and how advisors follow students. The answers tell you as much about fit as the meeting tells UNIS.

Practical details that matter

  • Book through the Ravenna calendar as soon as the application is in, and leave yourself time to sit the test before or after it.
  • Junior School tours are for adults only: for a young child, the first visit to the building is the evaluation itself, so talk about it beforehand.
  • Arrive early. The building is large, and a child who rushes in late starts on the back foot.
  • Do not over-prepare. A child who has been drilled shows it within minutes.

How I prepare a child for the UNIS visit

For the group activities, I run short collaborative tasks with feedback on listening and contributing. For the conversation, I hold mock interviews in English, recorded when the student agrees, and we work on concrete examples drawn from their own life, until they can talk about themselves the way they would to a teacher they like. The goal is never a performance; it is a student who is relaxed enough to be seen as they are.
