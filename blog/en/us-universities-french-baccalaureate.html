Do US universities accept the French baccalaureate? How a French file is read in America

United States October 2026 8 min read

Families' first question is almost always the same: "Is the French baccalauréat accepted in the United States?" The answer is yes, with no equivalence procedure. But the question is badly framed. An American university does not admit a diploma, it admits a student, and it reads that student through several years of school reports, a few letters and a personal file. The baccalauréat itself arrives after the decision.

Understanding how a French file is read over there changes how schooling is organised from the seconde (age 15 to 16) onwards. That is what this article covers; for the most selective universities, see also Getting into the Ivy League from France.

Is the French baccalaureate accepted in the USA?

Yes. American universities do not require any particular diploma. Harvard says so for all its applicants, whether they attend a school in the US or abroad: the requirements are the same, and there is no single expected path. What the university looks at is the difficulty of the curriculum: the strongest applicants are those who took the most demanding programme available to them.

Harvard adds advice that directly concerns French families: finish the curriculum you started. A student who completes only one year of IB or A levels before applying is, in its words, at a tremendous disadvantage. There is therefore no point leaving the baccalauréat for the IB in the final year in the hope of pleasing American universities. A demanding baccalauréat, done well, is better.

What universities read in a French file

The academic file goes through the school. The school counsellor, or a school leader, sends a report containing the transcripts, a recommendation letter and the school profile, the document that explains the grading system and the school's level. One or two teachers add their letters, depending on the university. During the year, the school sends a midyear report, then, after decisions, a final report.

This is where two difficulties specific to the French system come in. The 20-point scale first: a 15 out of 20 average, excellent in France, means nothing to an American reader unless someone explains it. It is not converted into a GPA; it is put in context, through the school profile and class rankings. Letters next: a French teacher rarely writes the detailed, personal letter an American university expects. Both can be prepared, and I go into them in the Ivy League article.

The French baccalaureate equivalent in the USA: choosing specialisms

There is no formal US equivalent of the baccalauréat; what matters is the difficulty of what the student chose. In the French system, students choose specialisms for their last two years. For a student aiming at engineering, computer science, economics or the sciences, the maths specialism through the final year, and ideally the maths expertes option, is the clearest sign of a demanding path. Harvard describes the ideal preparation as four years of maths, sciences, English and a foreign language, while making clear these are recommendations.

A humanities-minded student need not force maths if they are aiming for history or philosophy. But dropping maths at the end of the penultimate year closes many American doors, and that is decided a year earlier.

The baccalaureate arrives after the decision

American decisions come in December for early applications and in March or April for the rest. Baccalauréat results come out in July. Admission is therefore decided on school reports from the last three years of lycée, then the school sends a final report with the latest results. A drop at the end of the year shows.

One practical consequence: American applications and Parcoursup, the French national higher-education platform, are run in parallel. Nothing stops a student from listing French choices while waiting for American answers, and it is even wise, since most highly selective American universities admit a small fraction of their applicants.

A typical calendar for an application from a French lycée. SAT or ACT taken by the autumn of the final year at the latest. Early applications in early November, answers in December. Regular applications in early January, answers at the end of March. Parcoursup choices made in parallel, before the March deadline. Exact dates vary between universities and are checked every year.

Tests, English and cost

Several highly selective universities once again require the SAT or ACT. A good student in the French maths specialism can aim for the top of the scale in maths; the Reading and Writing section takes more work. I cover both in Taking the SAT from France and SAT Math from a French lycée. Some universities also ask for an English test from applicants whose language of instruction is not English: check university by university.

Then there is cost: at Harvard, over 90,000 dollars a year all-in for 2026-2027. The wealthiest universities nonetheless meet the full demonstrated need of admitted families, including international ones. The list of universities to target therefore also depends on their aid policy for international students.

Building an American application from a French lycée

An American application is not put together in the final year. Choice of specialisms, activities pursued over several years, test preparation, teachers' letters, essays: all of it is prepared between seconde and the summer before the final year. The families who succeed are those who understood early what an American university reads, and organised French schooling accordingly.
