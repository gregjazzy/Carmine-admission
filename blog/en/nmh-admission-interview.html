The NMH admission interview

Memorial Chapel, Northfield Mount Hermon, Massachusetts. Photo Daderot, Wikimedia Commons, CC0

US boarding schools November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

NMH interviews every applicant. There is no exemption for a strong transcript or a high score, and in a file where testing is optional, the interview is often the piece that tells the admission team most about how a child thinks. It is also the part families prepare least, because it looks like a conversation.

I prepare candidates for this interview every year, alongside the rest of the NMH file. Here is how it works and what makes a difference.

Booking: by phone or email, after the inquiry form

The interview is not booked in an online portal. The family first completes NMH's inquiry form, then calls or emails the admission office to schedule the interview, usually together with a campus tour. Tours run four days a week at set times, with two morning slots on Wednesdays, and last about an hour. Families who leave this until December find the calendar full and the January 15 deadline close.

For InitialView, the appointment must be made directly with that organization at least two weeks before January 15. The writing sample produced on the day is read by NMH's counselors next to the rest of the written application, so it carries real weight.

Ready for a real conversation

The NMH interview rewards a child who can think aloud and follow a conversation. I prepare candidates in English, in the format they will face, until they talk about what interests them with ease and substance.

Book a conversation

What the conversation is testing

NMH teaches in seventy-minute blocks of three courses per semester, built on discussion, labs and projects. A student spends most of the day talking with a teacher and a small group, not taking notes. The interview is the closest thing in the file to one of those classes: can the child explain something they care about, answer a question they did not expect, and build on what the adult says?

Children who interview well rarely have the most impressive activities. They have one or two subjects they know from the inside, a book, a project, a sport, an instrument, and they can talk about what is hard in it, what they changed their mind about, and what they want to do next. NMH's mission speaks of head, heart and hands; interviewers listen for all three.

Has your child ever spoken about themselves, in English, to an adult they do not know? Tell me about them.

The visit is part of the interview day

On campus, the tour is led by a current student who shows the dormitories, the Gilder Center for math and science and the Rhodes Arts Center. It is not formally assessed, but it gives the child things to say: a question asked in the dorm, a class seen through a door, something that surprised them. A child who arrives at the interview having noticed something specific is a child who has started to imagine being there.

Prepared, not scripted

Admission officers meet many coached candidates and recognize a memorized answer quickly: it is general, it is finished, and it cannot survive a follow-up. Good preparation works the other way. It finds the child's two or three real subjects, practises being interrupted and redirected, and gets the child comfortable saying that they do not know but would guess. For InitialView, it also means practising in front of a camera, where there is less warmth from the other side.

How I prepare candidates for the NMH interview

For seven years I have worked with around fifteen families a year aiming for the most selective boarding schools in New England, England and Switzerland. For the NMH interview, I work with the child in short sessions, in English and in the format they will face, on real conversations rather than scripts, and I help the family place the visit and the interview well before January.
