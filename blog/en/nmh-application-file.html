The NMH application file

A Northfield Mount Hermon application is not built in one place. The core file goes through one of two common platforms, the recommendations and transcripts follow their own route from the school, the interview is booked by phone, NMH's own supplement and later updates go through a parent portal, and an optional portfolio is sent by email. Families who see the whole map at the start lose far less time than those who discover it piece by piece.

I put these files together with families every year. They are set out below, in order.

Two platforms, one file

NMH accepts the Standard Application Online and Gateway to Prep Schools, with equal standing. The SAO, used by around 400 schools, gathers the family profile, the child's interests and achievements, student essays, parent statements, recommendations and transcripts, and flags each school's own instructions and supplements. Gateway reopens around July 1 for the following year.

The choice should follow the rest of the list. A family applying to several schools that use the SAO saves hours by writing the shared pieces once; a family whose other schools are on Gateway should stay there. NMH reads both the same way.

Two practical points. Test scores, including the English test, are accepted only when they come directly from the testing organization or its partners, so a PDF forwarded by the family does not count. And updated transcripts or new recommendations after submission go through the SchoolAdmin portal, not by email.

The portfolio: optional, and only as a link

Athletes and visual or performing artists may add supplementary material. NMH does not accept original artwork, CDs or DVDs; the family emails a link, with a short description of the submission in the subject line. A portfolio helps when it shows a level the rest of the file cannot convey: a short, well-chosen video of a performance or a match, a small selection of finished work. A long, unedited collection is more likely to go unwatched.

What makes the written part work

The essays and the parent statement are the only pieces the family writes. At a school that teaches in long seminar-style blocks, readers look for a child who notices things and thinks for themselves. One subject explored honestly says more than a list of achievements, which the recommendations and transcripts already provide. The parent statement is most useful when it describes how the child learns and what they still find hard, in terms that match what the teachers write.
