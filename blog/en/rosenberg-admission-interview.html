Institut auf dem Rosenberg admission interview: how to prepare

Institut auf dem Rosenberg, St. Gallen. Photo Rene Nueesch, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0

Book a conversation

At Rosenberg, the interview is not a formality after the exam. It is the moment the school checks that the applicant is the person their personal statement promised. I follow students at Rosenberg and the other leading Swiss boarding schools, and every year I prepare others for this meeting. What the school looks for, and how to prepare without turning it into a performance: that is what follows.

What the school looks for

Rosenberg expects internationally minded, creative students with strong academic potential and good social skills, able to show how their personality and talents will enrich the school and the world beyond. It wants them ambitious, ready to work to develop their talents, aware of their privilege, and focused on their own achievements rather than their family's legacy. The interview is there to see all of that up close.

The school publishes neither the questions, nor how it assesses, nor what tips the balance between two applicants with similar results. That is where preparation makes the difference.

Preparing for Rosenberg's interview

A child who talks about their project with accuracy, a personal statement that sounds like them: I prepare both, for Rosenberg as for the other boarding schools in view.

Topics to prepare

Without pretending to guess the questions, some topics inevitably come up.

What they would bring. Rosenberg wants to know what the child will make of the school, and what they will give the community. A talent, a project, an initiative already carried out count for more than a list of activities.

Their personal statement. Video, collage, text: whatever the form, the interview returns to it. A child should be able to extend it, not recite it.

Why Rosenberg. Classes of eight, teaching by level, the IDP, Talent & Enrichment: a child who knows what sets the school apart shows they can picture themselves there.

Does your child have a real project to tell? Tell me about them: I will tell you how to bring it out.

The costly mistakes

Highlighting the family rather than the child

Rosenberg wants students focused on their own achievements. An applicant who talks about their parents' name, network or travels more than about what they have done themselves sends exactly the wrong signal.

Learned answers

A child who recites is quickly spotted. The interview looks for a personality; a learned speech erases it.

A gap between the file and the person

A brilliant personal statement, obviously reworked by an adult, followed by a hesitant interview: the gap shows, and costs more than a simple but sincere statement.

How to prepare

Choose two or three moments in one's life that say something about oneself, and practise telling them in several ways. Work on spoken English around those exact topics. Prepare a real question about the school. And do mock interviews, so that on the day the child has already heard their own answers.
