Institut auf dem Rosenberg admission interview: how to prepare

Book a conversation

The Rosenberg admission interview is not a formality added to the file. It is the moment the school checks that the child matches the portrait drawn by their reports and personal statement. I have helped students win places at Rosenberg and Aiglon, and fifteen at Le Rosey, and the interview is where I see most good candidates give themselves away, often from preparing too much, or badly.

What the interview really tests

Rosenberg is not looking for compliant students. Its whole system rests on choice: close to 500 courses, a timetable unique to each student, an individual development plan from Grade 9. A child who does well but waits to be told what to do would be lost there. The interview first measures exactly that: can they explain what they love, why, and what they intend to do with it?

It then sets the person against the file. An enthusiastic statement about robotics falls flat if the child cannot describe a single project they have led. Conversely, a quiet file can come alive in front of a child who talks precisely about what fascinates them.

The ground a child must be ready to cover

I do not teach answers. I prepare the child for a few areas that an interview of this kind almost always comes back to.

Why Rosenberg rather than another Swiss boarding school. Families often hesitate between Le Rosey and Aiglon; the child should be able to say what, in this particular school, speaks to them: teaching by ability rather than by age, the freedom to shape their own path, the range of programmes.

What they want to do with their high school years. They do not need a career plan. They need a direction, and the words to explain it.

What they would bring to the school. Rosenberg sets great store by what its students do outside the classroom, with a dedicated talent and enrichment programme. Sport at a high level, a creative project, a long-term commitment all count, provided the child can say what they learned from it.

Life away from home. A boarding school takes in children who sometimes leave home very young. Showing that they have thought about it, without dramatising it, is reassuring.

The mistakes I see most

The first is recitation. A child running through a memorised script is spotted within thirty seconds, and the effect is disastrous at a school that values independent minds. The second is excessive modesty: many candidates, particularly from the French system, play down their best achievements. The third is a gap with the file, when an adult has polished the personal statement so much that the child no longer recognises themselves in it.

Finally, there is language. The school's programmes are taught in English, and a child who understands everything but struggles to speak about themselves with nuance in English loses part of what they have to say. This can be worked on, but not in a week.

Preparing without rehearsing

Good preparation does not manufacture a candidate. It helps a child put words to who they already are. It all starts with listening carefully to what really drives them; mock interviews follow, in French and then in English, until they speak about themselves naturally. The aim is for them to walk into the room knowing what they want to say, and to say it as they mean it.
