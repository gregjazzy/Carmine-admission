The Millfield interview

UK boarding schools November 2026 5 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

Whatever the age of entry, every pupil who joins Millfield has had an entrance interview with a senior member of staff. It lasts around thirty minutes and centres on three themes: the candidate's academic interests, their activities beyond lessons, and their experiences. Alongside the computer-based reasoning test and the school reference, it decides whether an offer follows, usually about a week later.

Academic interests

The question here is less "what are your grades?" than "what do you enjoy learning, and why?". A strong answer names a subject and goes into it: a topic that caught the child's attention, a book, a problem, something they looked up on their own. For a Sixth Form candidate, the link between this interest and the A levels or BTECs they plan to take matters too. A child who says "I like everything" has given the interviewer nothing to hold on to.

Activities beyond lessons

At a school with thirty sports, two hundred sports staff, an equestrian centre, a concert hall, a theatre and an art gallery, this part of the conversation carries real weight. The interviewer wants to understand what the child does, at what level, and with what commitment. Precision helps: the club, the position, the instrument and grade, the competitions, the hours. So does honesty about what is fun and what is serious.

Thirty minutes that count

I prepare children for the Millfield interview: finding what they truly have to say about their studies, their activities and their experiences, and saying it with ease to an adult they have just met, in person or on screen.

Experiences

This is the most open theme, and the one children prepare least. It can cover a move abroad, a family event, a project, a responsibility, a setback, a trip. What matters is what the child noticed and learned. A short story told with detail is worth more than a list of countries visited.

Would your child be at ease for thirty minutes with a stranger? Let me hear them.

On screen

The interview and assessment can be conducted remotely, on Zoom, which is how many families abroad take part. A remote interview needs its own preparation: a quiet room, a stable connection, the camera at eye level, and a child used to looking at the lens rather than at their own picture. Silences feel longer on screen, and a child who has practised online answers more naturally.

What weakens an interview

  • Answers learned by heart, which the interviewer recognises and steps around.
  • A parent answering, or prompting off-camera.
  • Talking only about sport, or only about marks, when the school is interested in both.

My approach

I have spent seven years preparing the children of around fifteen families a year for interviews at selective schools. For Millfield, I work through conversation rather than scripts: we find the subjects that bring the child to life, practise with a new adult, in person and online, and make sure each of the three themes has real material behind it.
