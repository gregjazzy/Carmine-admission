Math Tutoring for Franklin Students

Private Tutoring February 2026 10 min read

Franklin -- the Lycee International de Paris, housed within the Cite Scolaire Honore de Balzac in the 17th arrondissement -- holds a distinctive position in the Parisian educational ecosystem. It is a public school that offers high-level international sections, attracting students from expatriate and internationally mobile families seeking an anchor in the French system while maintaining a strong international dimension. But this hybrid positioning creates a precise challenge in mathematics: the program is entirely French, with all the rigor that implies, and students in international sections must conform to it without concession. A generic tutoring service, delivered by a tutor who understands neither this specific positioning nor the challenges it creates, is a waste of time and money.

With experience spanning over 1,600 students, including many from Franklin and other lycees with international sections, I know precisely where the friction points lie. The challenge is never a lack of talent. It is the gap between the mathematical habits the student has acquired in their prior education and what Franklin's French program demands every day.

What makes Franklin unique -- and demanding

Franklin is neither a classic French lycee nor an international school. It is a French public school with international sections -- in English, Arabic, Hebrew, Portuguese, Japanese, among others. Students follow the French curriculum in its entirety, with the addition of specific courses in their section language. Mathematics, physics-chemistry, history-geography, French -- all of this is taught in French, according to the national curriculum, without modification or accommodation for students in international sections.

This structure has direct implications for mathematics. A student in Franklin's English section follows exactly the same math program as a student in a standard French lycee in the 17th arrondissement. The same theorems, the same proofs, the same written assessments, the same standards for mathematical writing. The difference is that this student also manages the demands of their international section in parallel: literature courses in English, history in English, preparation for the specific BFI exams. The total workload is considerable, and mathematics, which requires regular and intensive practice, is often the first subject to suffer when time runs short.

The BFI (Baccalaureat Francais International) adds another layer of difficulty. BFI mathematics exams are more demanding than those of the standard bac, and the full suite of international section exams compounds an already dense program. Franklin students pursuing the BFI -- and this is the majority of those in international sections -- must excel in both dimensions: the French mathematics program and the specific demands of their section.

The specific difficulties of Franklin students

Franklin's student population is inherently diverse. Diplomat families, international organization executives, expatriates posted to Paris for a few years, binational families settled long-term. This diversity means students arrive at Franklin with highly varied mathematical backgrounds. Some come from the French system and feel at ease. Others arrive from American, British, or IB systems and face the same methodological shock as students arriving at Jeanine Manuel or Saint-Germain.

The methodological shock is the first challenge. French mathematics does not work like Anglo-Saxon mathematics. Where the American or British system asks students to find the right answer, the French system asks them to prove why the answer is correct. Where the IB system encourages exploration and investigation, the French system demands formalism and rigorous writing. A student who has always succeeded in math in an international system can find themselves failing at Franklin -- not because they do not understand math, but because they do not know how to express it in the French format. For an in-depth analysis of this gap, see our article on choosing between EJM, Franklin, and Saint-Germain for expatriate families.

The second challenge is managing the dual workload. Franklin students in international sections carry a significantly heavier workload than students at a standard French lycee. Section courses, foreign-language literature assignments, preparation for specific BFI exams -- all of this comes on top of the complete French program. Mathematics requires regular work to be mastered. A student who sacrifices their math practice to finish an English literature essay accumulates gaps that, after a few weeks, become difficult to close.

The third challenge is mathematical vocabulary. Franklin's international section students often think in their section language. In English, Portuguese, Japanese. French mathematics has a precise and dense vocabulary: "derivee," "primitive," "suite convergente," "demonstration par recurrence," "limite finie en l'infini." A student who knows the concepts but hesitates on the French vocabulary loses time on every exercise. This linguistic friction, invisible to parents and generic tutors, is a significant factor in underperformance.

The failure of generic tutoring

The 17th arrondissement is not short of math tutors. But virtually all of them are calibrated for students at standard French lycees. They do not understand the dual constraint of Franklin students -- a complete French program plus international section requirements. They cannot identify the specific methodological shock experienced by students from Anglo-Saxon systems. They do not measure the impact of bilingualism on mathematical performance.

A generic tutor will re-explain the math lesson and assign additional exercises. This is useful for a standard French student who needs reinforcement. It is insufficient for a Franklin student whose problem is methodological and linguistic before it is mathematical. What this student needs is a tutor who understands where they come from, what they can already do, and what specifically they are missing to succeed in the French format.

Physics and chemistry: an identical challenge

Difficulties in physics-chemistry at Franklin follow the same patterns as in mathematics. The physics-chemistry program is entirely French, with the same requirements for rigorous writing and formalism. A student from the English section who learned physics in a system where you "solve for x" without writing a complete scientific argument finds themselves penalized at Franklin, where every exercise demands a justified equation setup, a detailed resolution, and verification of the physical coherence of the result.

Chemistry amplifies these difficulties with its specific technical vocabulary. Organic nomenclature, redox equations, acid-base titrations -- all subjects where French vocabulary differs significantly from English vocabulary, and where a bilingual student must master both to perform in both contexts.
