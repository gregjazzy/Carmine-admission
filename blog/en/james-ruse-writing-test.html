The last section of the Selective High School Placement Test is the only one without multiple-choice answers. The child gets one writing task and thirty minutes. It counts for 25 percent of the result, exactly as much as Reading, Mathematical Reasoning or Thinking Skills, and it is the only one marked on a piece of the child's own writing.

Writing is also the skill I find most worth building before May, because it grows with practice and serves the child long after the test. Here is how the section works.

What is marked

The task gives a topic and instructions. Markers assess the creativity of the child's ideas and their ability to write effectively for a purpose and an audience, together with the use of language for effect: text structure, grammar, punctuation, style and vocabulary all contribute to the mark. One rule is stated without nuance: writing that does not address the given topic is marked lower, however fluent or creative it is.

That rule catches strong writers more than weak ones. A child with a well-practised story or a favourite opening in mind is tempted to bend the topic towards it. The result can be polished and still lose marks because it answers a different question.

Thirty minutes, on a keyboard

The response is typed into the test software. Children may plan on paper or on screen first. Two practical consequences follow. First, a child who rarely types loses time and fluency to the keyboard; a few weeks of regular typing before May removes that handicap. Second, thirty minutes includes the planning, so the child needs a habit of deciding in two or three minutes what they will say, to whom and in what order, before writing a word.

Building the skill

The Department's message is that the test needs no special knowledge, and for writing that is true: it rewards a child who writes often and reads well. What helps is regular, varied writing, stories, persuasive pieces, letters, short reflections, each read back by an adult who comments on one or two precise points rather than everything at once. Over months, children learn to open strongly, to hold one idea through a piece, to vary sentences and to choose words for their effect.

Timed practice comes later and in moderation. A handful of thirty-minute tasks in the official practice material, typed, in the weeks before the test, is enough to make the format familiar.
