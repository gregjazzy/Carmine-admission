Maths olympiads on a university application: what they are worth, from Parcoursup to Cambridge and MIT

Olympiads October 2026 7 min read

Parents ask me this in two ways. Before: "Is it worth pushing him towards the olympiads, for his application?" After: "She won a prize, how do we make the most of it?" Both questions overrate the line on the results list and underrate what produced it.

My view is simple. An olympiad prize carries little weight on its own, except at the very top. The training behind it carries a lot, because it prepares students for what the selection tests of the hardest programmes actually measure. Here is why, one admissions system at a time, for a student in the French system (a French lycée in France or abroad).

Maths olympiads on a Parcoursup application

Parcoursup is the French national platform for applying to higher education, including the classes préparatoires (prépas), the two-year intensive programmes that lead to the top engineering and business schools. It has no "awards" box. Competitions go in the optional section called Activités et centres d'intérêt (activities and interests), which the official student guide splits into four categories: leadership or supervising experience, civic engagement, work experience, and openness to the world. Each takes up to 1,500 characters, and the guide explicitly lists "participation in competitions" among the things worth mentioning. An olympiad naturally belongs under openness to the world.

Two practical points. The platform states that applicants must be able to prove any certificate they mention if the programme asks at enrolment, so keep the award or the email from the regional education authority. And a bare line ("Olympiades de première, regional prize") does less than three sentences on what the student worked on, for how long, and what it taught them. A maths teacher in a prépa reading the file knows the competition; what they want to find out is whether the student enjoys hard problems.

Keep the proportions in mind, too. Selective programmes read the school reports of the last two years of lycée first. A prize does not make up for a weak maths grade; it separates two files that are already strong.

Example: the 2026 Parcoursup round. The activities and interests section could be edited until 1 April 2026, 11:59 pm Paris time. The date changes every year and is published on parcoursup.gouv.fr.

Olympiads on a Cambridge or Oxford application

In the UK, everything goes through UCAS, and since the 2026 entry cycle the personal statement has been split into three questions, within an overall limit of 4,000 characters. The third asks what the student has done outside formal education to prepare for the course, and why it helps. That is where an olympiad belongs, as long as the student explains what it taught them rather than just that it happened.

For maths at Cambridge, though, the statement is not what decides. The faculty's admissions guide describes three filters: the TMUA, a written test now required of every applicant before interview invitations are sent; the interview itself, whose stated purpose is to see how the candidate thinks about a mathematical problem; and STEP, long problem papers sat in June, which form part of the conditional offer (often at least a grade 1 in both papers). Oxford also selects through a written test and interviews.

Read that list as a coach would: working alone on an unfamiliar problem, explaining your reasoning out loud, writing a complete solution against the clock. It describes olympiad training. Homerton, one of the Cambridge colleges, puts it its own way on its pages for prospective applicants: the British olympiad stretches the limits of your reasoning, and medals make great talking points in the personal statement. A student who has spent two years on past olympiad papers arrives at the TMUA and the interview with a head start that no results list measures.

Olympiads and MIT admissions

MIT is even clearer. Its page on STEM activities says in one sentence that it does not expect or require applicants to take part in any specific STEM programme, at MIT or elsewhere. It lists the major international olympiads, in maths, physics, chemistry, informatics and a few others, without making any of them a condition.

An admissions officer put it plainly on MIT's official blog: an olympiad medal is easy to verify and signals a rare level, but winning one does not guarantee admission, and not having one does not guarantee a rejection. What counts is the whole file: grades, tests, what the student has done with their curiosity. A French lycée student does not need an international medal to apply. They need to show that they do maths beyond the syllabus, and the French olympiads are the most legible proof of it.

The last row concerns a handful of students. For everyone else, the value lies in the first two columns: what the competition forced the student to learn, and how it is told. A British or American reader will not necessarily know the Olympiades de première. Say in one sentence what it is: a four-hour national paper of open-ended problems, for students entered by their school.

What matters more than the prize

I see two opposite mistakes. The first is entering a student in March of their penultimate year "for the application" without any preparation: they meet a type of problem they have never practised, come out discouraged, and the file gains nothing. The second is letting them prepare alone, working through past papers without anyone reading what they write.

The right calendar starts earlier. Olympiades de quatrième, the Coupe Animath in middle school, then the Olympiades de première: each stage teaches the student to search a little longer and write a little more cleanly. By the final year, a student aiming for a prépa, Cambridge or MIT has already done the work those programmes will test.
