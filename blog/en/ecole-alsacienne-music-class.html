École alsacienne: the music class, another way into sixième

The École alsacienne, Paris 6th. Photo Celette, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Paris schools November 2026 5 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

Of the sixty or so places the École alsacienne opens in sixième, about fifteen are reserved for its music class, the "classe à horaires aménagés musique" (CHAM), a class with an adapted timetable for young musicians. For a family whose child plays an instrument, it is tempting to see it as an easier door. It rarely is. But for the child who truly has the profile, it is one of the finest programmes in Paris.

I have students at the Alsacienne, and every year I see families considering this route, for good reasons and bad ones.

A class apart, from sixième to troisième

The music class has about twenty-four students, while the school's other collège classes have twenty-eight to thirty. The timetable is concentrated in the mornings and early afternoon, leaving the end of the day for schoolwork and music. A few subjects have fewer hours, in sport, history-geography and experimental sciences; music education is reinforced, focused on culture, styles and works. And unlike what national rules provide, the school lets these students take the same options as the others: bilingual English, Latin, the two-language class, Russian.

Coming in through the door that suits them

I help you judge whether the music class is the right door for your child, then prepare them for everything beyond the audition that decides their place: the application, the test and the interview. My aim: that they get into the École alsacienne by the route where they are strongest.

What the school requires

To join the music class, the child must be enrolled at a music school, a municipal or national conservatoire, a public music school or the Schola Cantorum, and follow three courses there: an instrument, music theory and a group practice, which can be done at the school, in its chamber music workshop or its rock band. They commit to continuing until the end of troisième: a certificate is requested every year. And all applicants, including students already at the school, have an audition with a music teacher, followed by an interview.

Why it is not a shortcut

The mistake I see most often is thinking that music replaces the rest. It does not. The school record is still read, and for entry into sixième the school assesses all applicants in French and maths, with the same attention to consistent results. The audition is added to this selection; it does not bypass it. A child whose academic level is borderline, counting on their violin to make up for it, often has less chance through this door than through the other.

The other mistake is underestimating the commitment. Four years of conservatoire alongside a demanding collège, with afternoons given over to music: it is a very full life for a student. The children who thrive there are those for whom music is already a passion, not an application argument. For them, the reward is real: high-level music training at one of the best schools in Paris and, at the end of troisième, access to the school's general seconde or to a dual-track seconde at another school.

How I prepare music class applicants

The audition belongs to your child's music teachers. Everything else, I prepare with them: an assessment at the school's level, in French, maths and English, to see whether their record holds up against other applicants'; preparation for the test; the interview after the audition, where they must talk about their music with insight and without reciting; the application and calendar; and the list of other collèges to aim for, with or without an adapted timetable. For seven years, I have supported around fifteen families a year in this way with their admissions to the leading Paris schools.
