Applying to Hotchkiss as a homeschooled student

Hotchkiss states that homeschooling has a strong place in today's education and gladly accepts applications from qualified homeschooled students. The process is the standard one, with a formal application and all required standardized testing, plus a set of documents designed to let the committee understand an education it cannot read through a school transcript.

What homeschooled applicants add

Why the interview is mandatory here

For other applicants the interview is recommended; for homeschooled students it is required before review. The reason is practical: without classmates, grades from a school or teachers who compare the student with peers, the committee needs to meet the student to judge readiness for a demanding boarding community. The same qualities Hotchkiss names for everyone, character, independence and citizenship, have to come through in conversation.

Making the program legible

The statement of philosophy and the syllabi do the work a transcript does for other applicants. They should be specific: which texts were read, which mathematics was covered and with which materials, how writing was taught and assessed, what external courses or competitions the student took part in. A committee that can see the level of the work can compare it; a vague narrative cannot be compared with anything.

Standardized tests carry particular weight for the same reason: they give the committee a common measure. Homeschooled students should prepare them as seriously as the documents.

Recommendations from outside the family

When a parent is the main teacher, Hotchkiss accepts that parent's recommendation forms, and encourages additional letters from other adults involved: tutors, online course instructors, coaches, music teachers, mentors. A letter from someone outside the family, who has seen the student work alongside others, often helps the committee most.

Testing as the common yardstick

For a homeschooled student, the SSAT or ISEE (or, for older applicants, the SAT, ACT or PSAT) is the one measure the committee can place directly beside every other applicant. That argues for taking it early, in October or November, with time for a second sitting in December, and for preparing the sections that a home curriculum may not have practised under time pressure: verbal reasoning, reading speed and timed mathematics. A strong, well-prepared score turns a program the committee has to interpret into one it can trust.

The same logic applies to outside evidence. A course taken with an online provider, a regional mathematics competition, a music exam with an external board: each gives the committee a reference point that a family narrative cannot.
