From sixth grade, two pieces of a Sidwell Friends application show the child through other eyes than a test: a short video they record at home, and confidential recommendations from their current math and English teachers. Both have strict timing, and both reward something other than polish.

I work on these pieces with families every year. Here is how they work.

The applicant video

The video is a short recording made by the applicant at home, so the committee can see and hear them directly, in their own words, space and pace. The admissions team sends the prompts students choose from after the Family Information Form is submitted, and the video is due by December 31. The school's reasoning is that not every applicant interviews with ease in a formal live setting; a home video lets a student's own voice come through without that pressure.

The school is explicit that there is no need to script every word or to retake until it is perfect: natural is more useful than rehearsed. Good preparation, then, is not writing a speech. It is helping the child choose the prompt they have something real to say about, think through two or three points, and speak simply.

Our experience with Sidwell Friends

We have a track record of admissions to Sidwell Friends, and we put that experience at your family's disposal: a student who knows what they want to say on camera and says it naturally, and teachers briefed in good time.

The recommendations

RuleDetail
WhoCurrent math and English teachers (Grades 6, 7, 9); the current teacher or caregiver for younger children
WhenRequest after November 1; forms received before are declined; due December 15
Extra lettersNot accepted
ContentHow the child engages with material, works with peers and handles challenges in class

The November 1 rule exists so that teachers write about a child they have taught for a real part of the year. The school advises asking teachers in person if possible and giving them lead time: a request in early November completed in early December is far easier than one made on December 10.

What ties them together

The video and the recommendations describe the same child from two angles, one in their own voice and one in a teacher's. When the video shows a student talking with interest about something, and the teachers describe a student who engages and works well with others, the file reads as consistent. That consistency matters more than any single piece.
