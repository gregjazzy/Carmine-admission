Applying to Choate from abroad

About 18% of Choate's students are international, and they hold passports from around sixty countries. The school runs admission receptions abroad every winter: this year in Montreal, Seoul, Tokyo and Taiwan. For a family outside the United States, the process is the same as for everyone else, with four points that deserve particular care: status, English, money and the interview.

Who counts as international

The first reading of a file looks at where the child goes to school. A student at a school outside the United States identifies as an international applicant on Gateway. The committee then also considers citizenship, and the two do not always line up. An American child at school in London may be read as a domestic applicant. A child without US citizenship who lives in Boston may be read as international. Families with dual nationality or a recent move should not assume which category they fall into.

The admission team is trained to evaluate a wide range of international curricula. That is reassuring, but it does not mean every transcript speaks for itself: a reader can only credit what the documents make clear.

The test is strongly recommended for non-native speakers and for students who have spent fewer than two years in a school where English is the language of instruction, taken from June of the application year. A TOEFL of 100 is an academic level. For a fourteen-year-old schooled in another language, reaching it is usually a matter of months, not weeks.

Transcripts from another system

Choate asks for current grades and those of the last two years, with a counselor recommendation. A school abroad often has its own grading scale, its own report format and no habit of writing American-style recommendations. What helps: a school profile that explains the scale, a counselor who places the child in their class, and, where the grades are hard to compare, a test score. Choate is test-optional, but for a student from a system the committee sees less often, a strong SSAT is often the clearest way to show the level.

Financial aid without a US tax return

International families can apply for aid. Those who do not file a US return complete the Clarity application in US dollar equivalents and upload the previous year's official income and asset documents: tax returns, wage statements, an employer's letter. Choate meets 100% of demonstrated need, but when the total need of the pool exceeds the budget, need can weigh in the admission decision. A family abroad asking for substantial aid should know that the rest of the file has to be particularly strong.

Scholar programs with an international reach

Two of Choate's named programs are aimed at students from abroad. The Gakio-Walton International Scholars, created in 2006, identifies one or two students a year, primarily from Kenya or continental Africa, with full aid plus books, supplies and travel home for the four major breaks. The Murata US-Japan program funds talented Japanese students for a summer and an academic year; those who want to stay until graduation reapply for admission. Neither has a separate application: recipients are identified during the admission review.

The interview from abroad

Every Choate interview is online, so no family travels for it. That removes a cost, and it adds a difficulty: the child speaks for twenty to twenty-five minutes alone with the interviewer, in English, on a screen, sometimes late in the evening because of the time difference. It is worth choosing a slot at a reasonable hour for the child, and practising on the same kind of call beforehand.
