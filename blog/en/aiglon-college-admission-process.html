Aiglon College: admission, from the visit to the decision

Villars-sur-Ollon, home of Aiglon. Photo Bledard92, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Boarding schools November 2026 5 min read

Book a conversation

Aiglon describes its admissions process as "selective, yet untraditional". The phrase is accurate. The school is not looking for the best academic record; it is looking for a child able to follow a demanding curriculum, thrive in a mountain environment and live in a community of more than sixty-five nationalities. And it judges every applicant against five principles: challenge, respect, responsibility, diversity and service.

Five steps

It all starts with a short enquiry, after which the admissions team opens a conversation with the family. Next comes the visit, strongly recommended: an initial ninety-minute visit to discover the school, then, once the application is submitted, a full admissions visit of three to four hours, which includes the assessments and the interview. The application itself brings together the last two years of school reports, the contact details of two teachers who will send confidential recommendations on the child's academic level and character, a copy of the passport and the registration fee.

The admissions committee decides every month, with three possible outcomes: acceptance, the waiting list, which is reviewed again each month, or a decline, with personalised feedback for the parents.

An application that looks like your child

What changes with age

For the youngest, in Years 3 and 4, the child has an "experience" at the school: attending a lesson, playing at break, writing a short piece, reading and doing a short maths quiz. From Year 5 to Year 11, they have an interview and online assessments covering problem solving, mathematical reasoning, logic and literacy; everything can be done remotely, though the school encourages a visit. For Year 12, the start of the International Baccalaureate, an in-person visit is compulsory, with an additional maths test using a calculator.

The interview, the real moment of truth

The school says it wants the interview to get to know the child: their character, studies, interests, friendships, their view of boarding life, what excites them. What I see is that this is where most is decided. A child who talks about themselves with insight, who shows they want expeditions, community life and responsibility, has a decisive advantage over an applicant with similar grades who recites what they think is expected.
