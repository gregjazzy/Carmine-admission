Aiglon College: admission, from the visit to the decision

Villars-sur-Ollon, home of Aiglon. Photo Bledard92, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

At Aiglon, admission is not won in a competitive exam. The Villars school describes its own process as selective yet untraditional: it looks at whether a child could access its curriculum, thrive in the mountains and find their place in an international community. I have helped students win places at Aiglon and Rosenberg, and fifteen at Le Rosey, and every year I see families misread this process, for want of knowing what the school is looking for at each stage.

What the school is really judging

Aiglon assesses every applicant against its Guiding Principles and five pathways: challenge, respect, responsibility, diversity and service. These are not brochure words. Since John Corlette founded it in 1949, the school has set out to develop mind, body and spirit together, with as much life outdoors as in the classroom. A brilliant child who dislikes physical effort or community life can be less at home there than a more average student who fully shares that spirit.

The journey, from enquiry to answer

It starts with an enquiry, followed by a conversation with the admissions team about the family's needs and goals. Then comes the visit, which the school strongly recommends: an initial visit of about an hour and a half, or a full admissions visit of three to four hours, which includes the interview and assessments and can only be booked once the application is in.

The application is completed on the admissions portal: school reports for at least the last two years, contact details for two teachers at the current school, who will write confidential recommendations on the child's work and character, a scan of the passport, and a non-refundable registration fee of CHF 3,000.

Recommendations, the overlooked piece

Two current teachers write directly to the school, and the family never reads their letters. It is often the most decisive part of the file, and the one families think about least. A teacher who barely knows the child, or does not know what a school like Aiglon expects, will write a polite, flat letter. Choosing the two teachers, and how to approach them, should be planned months ahead.

Not sure which teachers to ask? Let's talk: the choice matters more than people think.

The interview, where everything comes together

The interview covers the child's character, academics, interests, friendships and boarding life. The school wants to understand where their enthusiasm lies and how their talents fit their plans. It is the moment when reports, recommendations and assessments acquire a face. A child who talks about themselves accurately, without reciting, gives coherence to everything else. Conversely, a child who has been coached into a polished script sounds like every other candidate in the room, and the committee notices it straight away. Families applying from abroad should also know that, outside Year 12, the interview can take place online. For the assessments themselves, see should you prepare for the Aiglon assessments.

Three possible answers

The Enrolment Committee reviews applications and communicates decisions monthly. The answer is one of three: an offer of a place, a place on the waiting list, or a decline, with personalised feedback for the parents.

The waiting list deserves attention. It does not mean the child has failed: the school places there candidates it sees as promising but cannot take straight away, because of limited space in a year group, a pathway or a boarding house, or because other applications were stronger. These files are reviewed again every month. Knowing how to handle that wait, and preparing a fallback, is part of my work.
