Institut auf dem Rosenberg fees: the cost of a year, and what it covers

Institut auf dem Rosenberg, St. Gallen. Photo Rene Nueesch, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0

Swiss boarding schools November 2026 5 min read

Rosenberg may well be the most expensive school in the world, and it does not mind the label. For a family it is not. A year in St. Gallen costs on average around CHF 165,000 per student. That figure is not a price list, it is an average, and the whole logic of the school lies in that nuance.

Two parts, one of which varies

The fee is made up of a base fee, CHF 100,000 per school year, and individual fees of around CHF 65,000 on average. The base fee covers tuition, all regular meals, non-chargeable co-curricular and athletics courses, and the IDP, the individual development plan around which each student's schooling is organised. Individual fees depend on the life the child leads: the room category in each residence, health insurance, special dietary needs, laundry, private tuition and personal learning modules, examination fees, school trips and excursions, chargeable sports or arts options.

There is no all-inclusive fee, and that is deliberate. Rosenberg prefers à la carte to a set menu: a student on a highly individualised programme, with a personal trainer or tailored modules, will go well above the average; another will stay below it.

A place cannot be bought

At this price, many families assume admission follows. It does not: Rosenberg turns down strong applicants every year. I prepare applications that stand out from the first reading, and children ready for the interview and the entry exams.

What the fee does not buy

The school receives more strong applications every year than it has places, and deliberately limits enrolment. The application itself costs CHF 1,000, non-refundable, before any decision. Scholarships are all but non-existent: Rosenberg receives no government subsidy, accepts no donations, and its few scholarship places are reserved years ahead. In 2026, none were available.

For a family, the budget is the condition, never the argument. What gets a child in is the application, their personality as it comes through in the personal statement and the interview, their entry exam results, and the moment the application arrives.

The budget is in place; the application remains. Tell me about your child: I will tell you whether Rosenberg is the right school for them.

What really decides

I have students at Rosenberg as at Le Rosey and Aiglon, and every year I support around fifteen families towards the most selective schools and boarding schools. What I often see in families aiming for Rosenberg is preparation focused on the wrong thing: prices are compared, visits planned, residences debated, and the personal statement written the night before. Yet that statement, with the interview, is what makes the difference. I prepare applications of excellence, showing a project and a personality rather than a status, and children who reach the exams at the top of their level.

Rosenberg against the other leading boarding schools

Le Rosey and Aiglon have more readable annual fees, with fewer individual lines. Comparing raw figures therefore makes little sense: at Rosenberg, the average includes choices that elsewhere would be billed separately or would not exist, such as a timetable built around the student and classes of eight. The real comparison is about which child each school serves best, and that is the subject of Rosenberg, Le Rosey or Aiglon.
