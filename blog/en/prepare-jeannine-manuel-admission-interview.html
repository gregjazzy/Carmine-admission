Preparing for the Jeannine Manuel Admission Interview

Photo: École Jeannine Manuel

EJM Admission February 2026 · updated November 2026 5 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. Has worked with students of École Jeannine Manuel for more than ten years.

Book a conversation

Of all the stages of admission to École Jeannine Manuel, the interview is the one families worry about most, and the one they prepare worst: either not at all, or by having the child learn answers by heart. I have worked with students of the school for more than ten years, and every year I prepare others for this meeting. Here is what the school says about it, and how to prepare without turning it into a recital.

Who is interviewed, and how

The interview takes a different form depending on age. Up to CM2 (age 10), it is mainly the parents who are interviewed: about half an hour with the director of admissions or a member of the leadership team, and the school asks for both parents to attend, even if separated. The child takes part in small-group activities in the early years, then a short academic assessment from CE1 to CM2 (ages 7 to 10). In sixième (age 11), the school meets the candidates and their parents. From cinquième (age 12), the student attends the interview with their parents, and only some candidates are invited, after the written tests. In lycée, the school meets some candidates with their parents.

For a family still living abroad, the interview can take place in London, in New York or remotely.

Preparing for the Jeannine Manuel interview

A child who talks simply about themselves, parents who know what they have come to say: I prepare both, for the Jeannine Manuel interview and for those of the backup schools.

What the school says it looks for

The interview is as much the parents' as the child's. The school writes that it expects families to have a strong international outlook, to support its mission and to be closely involved in their children's education and well-being. On the student side, it looks for children who will benefit from a demanding bilingual curriculum, and between two equivalent applications it takes interests and talents outside school into account.

It publishes neither the questions, nor the assessment grid, nor what tips the balance between two candidates. That is where preparation makes the difference.

Maturity, perspective, curiosity: none of this can be improvised the night before. Tell me about your child and I will tell you how to prepare their interview.

Topics to prepare

Without pretending to guess the questions, some topics inevitably come up when a child talks about themselves. It is better to have thought about them beforehand.

  • Their background. Schools, countries, moves: not as a list, but with what each one taught them.
  • Why this school. "Because it is a good school" says nothing. A child who knows that teachers teach in their own language, that a third of the students are non-French speakers, or that in lycée they will choose between the BFI and the IB, shows a real interest.
  • What genuinely interests them. A book, a passion, a project. Talking in detail about one thing they love is worth more than listing ten.
  • For older students, what comes next. A teenager does not need a career plan, but should be able to say what draws them.

I work through these questions with your child in mock interviews, in French and in English. Prepare their interview.

The mistakes that cost dearly

Recited answers

A child who has learned answers by heart is quickly spotted: the tone is flat, and they lose their footing as soon as the question changes. A child who takes a second to think is better than one who recites.

Parents speaking for the child

When a twelve-year-old uses adult vocabulary and cannot develop an idea beyond the prepared sentence, it is clear who wrote the speech. The effect is the opposite of the one intended.

Parents who have not prepared

The parents' interview matters. The school expects both of them, and it looks at their plans for their child. Arriving without having thought about what you expect from the school, or contradicting each other, leaves a poor impression.

How to prepare

Tell, do not recite. The child picks three or four moments from their background that say something about them, and practises telling them in different ways, so they can use them whatever the question.

Talk about yourself in both languages. The school is bilingual and requires English from CE1. A child at ease talking about themselves in English as well as in French arrives calmer.

Work on presence. Eye contact, how to sit, how to say hello. This is practised in mock interviews, not on the morning itself.

Have a real question. A child or parent who asks a precise question about the school, the choice between the BFI and the IB, or an activity that interests them, shows they can see themselves there.
