Why have your child learn Chinese? What the language trains, and what it sorts

Languages October 2026 7 min read

“What use will it be if my child never lives in China?” Families ask me this every time they hesitate over Chinese for their child. It is a fair question, and the usual answer, the world's second-largest economy and the careers that come with it, has never convinced me for a ten-year-old. Careers are a long way off. What Chinese does to the way a pupil works, on the other hand, shows from the first year.

What Chinese trains, beyond Chinese

I see these four qualities in pupils who have done several years of Chinese, and they show first outside Chinese: in how a maths paper is laid out, in patience with a long exercise, in the way a new topic is approached without panic.

A rare language, and that is the point

Chinese is still taught very little in France. According to the DEPP, the statistics department of the French Ministry of Education, 10,900 pupils in general and technological lycées (upper secondary, ages 15 to 18) studied it as a first or second foreign language at the start of the 2024 school year, and 7,950 as a third language. For comparison, three quarters of the pupils in those lycées study Spanish.

That rarity has an effect families rarely notice at the start. A Chinese class, in collège (lower secondary, ages 11 to 15) or lycée, brings together pupils whose parents made a demanding choice, and who accepted a subject where there is no faking the work. Those who keep going with characters for four or five years are very often the ones who keep going everywhere. This is what I tell families: Chinese works as a filter, and the filter works in favour of the child who comes through it, because they end up among strong pupils and their record marks them out as one.

On a school report, the line “Chinese” counts mainly for what it says about the pupil: several years of steady effort on a subject that is thankless at first, with results to show. Chinese dropped halfway through does not tell the same story.

Start in collège, not in lycée

The DEPP figures show a trend worth taking seriously. As a third language in lycée, Chinese has collapsed: 7,950 pupils in 2024 against 14,100 in 2020. Since the reform of the baccalauréat, the French school-leaving exam, a third language is only an optional extra on top of the specialist subjects, and many pupils give it up. As a first or second language, by contrast, Chinese is growing: 10,900 pupils against 9,500.

The practical conclusion is simple. The Chinese that lasts is the Chinese started early, in collège, as a second language, and carried through to the bac. Chinese started at fifteen as one more option is often the first thing sacrificed when the final year fills up. For Paris, I have gathered the schools that teach it, and how to get in, in Chinese in Paris middle schools.

What it is worth in an application

For a selective admission, in France or abroad, Chinese counts in two ways. First through the regularity it demonstrates, on school reports that cover several years. Then through an outside measure: the HSK, the official Chinese proficiency exam, which places the pupil's level on a recognised scale, independent of their mark in class. A fourteen-year-old who has passed a first HSK level shows something verifiable, which weighs more than a language line on a report.

Starting at home

Many families have no school offering Chinese within reach, or want their child to get a head start before secondary school. We built Xiaomo, our Chinese app, for them: one short session a day, a story that carries on from one session to the next, and every character traced with a finger, because that is where the care I described above is built. HSK levels 1 and 2 are complete, and it is free, with no sign-up. You will find it in our resources.

One piece of advice to finish: a quarter of an hour every day is worth more than an hour on Sunday. Chinese rewards regularity more than any other subject I know.
