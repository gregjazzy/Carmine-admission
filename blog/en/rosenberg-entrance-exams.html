Institut auf dem Rosenberg entrance exams: what you need to know

Institut auf dem Rosenberg, St. Gallen. Photo Rene Nueesch, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0

The Rosenberg entrance exam is the step families prepare for least. They think about the fees, the personal statement, the visit to St. Gallen; the exam comes last in their minds, yet it is often what decides. I have helped students win places at Rosenberg and Aiglon, and fifteen at Le Rosey, and every year I prepare candidates for the entrance tests of the leading Swiss boarding schools. Behind the brochure, this exam looks rather different from what families expect.

An exam only for files already shortlisted

Everything starts with an online file: report cards for the last three years, a personal statement, a scan of the passport, any document that supports the application, and an application fee of CHF 1,000 per candidate. Only applicants who pass this first reading are invited to an interview and to sit the entrance exams.

The child who sits the papers has therefore already convinced on paper. The candidates around them have strong reports, often from well-known international schools. The exam is not there to weed out weak students: it separates strong ones, and a merely decent result is enough to lose the place. Afterwards, the school makes either a direct offer or a conditional one, and the family has only a few days to accept it by settling the first invoice.

A school that does not think in years

Rosenberg places students by ability, not by age. In middle school, the core subjects, mathematics and English included, are taught at four levels; in high school, close to 500 courses lead to IGCSEs, the IB, APs, A-levels or the school's own diploma, and every student has a personal timetable, built from Grade 9 onwards with an individual development plan.

For the exam, this changes everything. It does not only check that a thirteen-year-old is at the level of their age: it also helps decide where to place them, and which level of maths or English they will start in. A candidate who scrapes through enters at the bottom, and will feel it for a long time. Neither the exact subjects of the papers, nor their length, nor the expected bar appear anywhere in the procedure. Those details are what make the difference, and they are what I know, from preparing candidates for Swiss boarding schools and following students at the school.

Where very good students stumble

The first trap is written English. The IB, A-levels and APs are all taught in English. A child schooled in French, German or Russian can shine in conversation and still be far from the writing expected of a future student on these tracks. It is the gap I see most often.

Will their written English hold up? Tell me about your child: I measure it at the level of the top boarding schools.

The second is mathematics. A top student at a French lycée often masters formal proof but has never worked fast, in English, on IGCSE-style questions; a student from an international school calculates well but rarely justifies. A school that streams students by level spots these gaps quickly.

The third is believing the exam stands on its own. At Rosenberg, results are read alongside the whole profile: the personal statement, which can take any form, video or collage included, and the interview. A candidate who does honestly well but shows neither what drives them nor what they would bring to the school gives way to a more distinctive profile.

Interview and personal statement, one story

Rosenberg looks for students who aim high, are open to the world, and build their success on their own rather than on their family's name. That sentence deserves to be taken seriously. An interview where a child lists activities without saying what they learned from them rings hollow, and a brilliant statement that the interview does not confirm weakens the whole application. I work on both together, so that they tell the same story about your child, in their own words.

The right moment to start

A campus visit is only offered once the file is complete. Your child therefore needs to be ready for the papers when the application goes in, not a few weeks later. Closing a gap in written English or maths takes several months: I advise starting two to three terms before the target entry, and submitting the file as soon as your child is ready.
