The three Deerfield essays

Part II of the Deerfield application belongs to Deerfield alone. It sits inside Gateway, but no other school asks for this combination: a short answer, a personal essay and a graded essay. The first two are written for the application, on prompts the child chooses. The third already exists, somewhere in the child's schoolwork this year.

The short answer and the personal essay

For both, the child picks the questions that interest them most from Deerfield's prompts. The school asks for their perspective, their voice and their hopes for the future, and welcomes a straightforward or a creative approach. That freedom is useful and also a test: choosing a prompt is the first decision the reader sees.

Three habits make these pieces work. Choose the prompt the child has something specific to say about, not the one that sounds most impressive. Start from a real moment, conversation or object rather than a statement about character. And let the child sound fourteen: Deerfield will also read the SSAT or ISEE writing sample, produced under time pressure without help, and a large gap between the two is noticed.

The graded essay: the heart of Part II

This piece must come from one of the child's current academic courses and must include the teacher's comments. Deerfield explains exactly what it is looking for: how the student engages with writing and revision, how they respond to feedback, think critically and develop ideas. And then the sentence families should read twice: a paper that shows thoughtful teacher commentary and the student's effort to improve is more valuable than one that simply received a high grade.

Three pieces chosen and written with care

I read the child's current schoolwork to choose the graded essay, then work with them on the short answer and personal essay, draft after draft, without ever writing for them.

How to choose the graded paper

Look for comments that engage. A margin that says "good" teaches the reader nothing. A teacher who questions an argument, asks for evidence or suggests a different structure gives Deerfield exactly what it wants to see.

Look for revision. A paper that went through drafts, where the second version visibly answers the first round of comments, is the ideal case.

Do not choose by grade. It is the family's reflex, and it is the opposite of Deerfield's instruction. A B+ paper with a real dialogue with the teacher can beat an A with nothing in the margin.

Choose from a current course. Last year's best essay does not qualify. Families who think about this only in December may find that the current term has produced few long papers; it is worth knowing in October what is coming.

For a child taught in another language

A student at a school that does not teach in English may have no graded English paper with real comments, or only short exercises from an English class. The choice then becomes delicate: a substantial paper in the language of instruction, or a shorter one in English. There is no single answer; it depends on what each paper shows and on whether the reader can follow it. It should be thought through early, not decided the week of the deadline.

What not to do

Do not rewrite the graded paper; it must be the paper the teacher graded. Do not polish the personal essay until it sounds like an adult. Do not send extra writing samples in the hope of strengthening the file: most applicants do not need supplemental material, and Deerfield warns that excessive submissions are generally unnecessary.
