GPA conversion for French grades: why US colleges read the 20-point scale as it is

International Admissions October 2026 8 min read

French schools grade out of 20, and a 14 average is a good result. Families looking at American universities soon want to know what that is as a GPA, and the internet obliges with conversion tables, none of which agree. The universities' own answer is less convenient: most of them do not want you to convert at all, and the most selective say so in writing. A few do recalculate, using their own rules, and the result is never the figure an online converter gives you.

What admissions offices actually say

Stanford's page for international applicants states that there is no need to have marks converted into an American GPA, and that applicants are evaluated within their own country's education system. Penn's FAQ says international transcripts do not need third-party evaluation and that grades, GPAs and test scores should not be recalculated. Princeton does not expect the applicant or the school to provide a conversion or a GPA if that is not standard practice in the school's system.

Yale does not even publish an average GPA. In its 2025-2026 Common Data Set, the standard questionnaire US universities fill in each year, the GPA sections are left blank. And only 31% of its incoming first-years had a class rank to report.

One 14, three answers

Conversion tables contradict each other because each rests on a different assumption about what a French grade is worth. Take three common marks.

Between the first row and the third, the same 14 goes from a weak average to a perfect one. Straight proportion is the most misleading, because French grading barely uses the top of its scale: ENSTA notes that marks above 16 are traditionally rare. The generous tables know this and shift everything upward, but each picks its own shift.

That is why the common questions have no stable answer. What is a 4.0 GPA in France? Depending on the table, 14, 16 or 20. What is a 3.5? By proportion, 17.5; by the ENSTA table, around 12. A family building a college list on either figure is building on sand.

How a reader handles a French report card

They read it as it is, next to two other documents: the school profile and the counselor letter. How those documents travel through the application is covered in The Common App from a French lycée; what matters here is what they say about grades.

French report cards, the bulletins, have a feature people forget. For each subject they show the class average, and often the highest and lowest marks in the class. A 15 in maths in a class averaging 10.5 reads at once, to anyone, without a conversion table. A GPA wipes out exactly that context.

College Essay Guy, a site run by an American counselor, surveyed admissions officers on the subject. About half of the institutions recalculate grades internally, the other half never do, and around 60% of respondents said that if GPA is optional, leave it blank and they will work it out. One officer put the risk plainly: translating grades yourself often results in a mismatched evaluation of academic potential.

A university that recalculates uses its own scale, so a figure supplied by the family is of no use to it. A university that does not recalculate reads the transcript, so the figure is of no use there either. Either way, a home-made GPA adds nothing and can cast doubt on the rest of the file.

Universities that do require a recalculation

Some universities do want a number, but none of them asks the family to produce it. The University of Florida requires, for all coursework completed outside the US, a course-by-course credential evaluation with a GPA calculation from a member of NACES, the National Association of Credential Evaluation Services. The candidate does not calculate anything; the evaluator does, by its own method.

WES, the best-known evaluator, is often described as something only graduate applicants need. That is wrong: its evaluations page covers first-year (freshman) and transfer admission too. But it is only useful where a university asks for it, and selective private universities, Penn among them, do not. I would pay for an evaluation only for a university that explicitly requires one, and only for that university.

The University of California is a case of its own. It asks international first-year applicants for fifteen academic year-long courses with a 3.4 GPA, and has them enter their grades from their transcript. It publishes no table for France; it does the calculation itself.

Class rank, and what the school can say instead

Rank is not a required piece: at Yale, fewer than a third of new students had one to report. Most French lycées do not rank their students. The profile of Lycée Claudel in Ottawa, a school in the AEFE network of French schools abroad, says it plainly: no credits per course, no ranking, and most universities know the 20-point scale and read the report cards as they are.

A school that does not rank should not invent a rank. What it can do, and what helps, is place the student in the counselor letter: in the top tenth of the final-year class, highest mark in the class in physics and chemistry over the year. Sentences like these do the work of a rank, provided the school is willing to write them; teacher and counselor letters are covered in Recommendation letters from French teachers.

What a French lycée's school profile should contain.

A sentence on the 0 to 20 scale and how rarely marks above 16 are given. The distribution of averages in the final-year class, or failing that the distribution of baccalauréat honours (mentions) over three or four years. The specialty subjects the student took and their weekly hours. A statement that the school ranks, or does not. And if the school wants to give letter-grade guidance, a table of its own that it stands behind, rather than one found online.

And if the form asks for a GPA?

I keep to a few principles. If the university does not ask for a GPA, do not give one. If the form has an optional field and the school does not calculate a GPA, leave it blank. If the university requires a number, it says how to get one, through an evaluation service or its own entry form, and you follow that method to the letter. Calculating a converted GPA yourself is never the right answer.

In the end, what decides is whether the file is readable: a school profile that explains the grading, a counselor letter that places the student, specialty grades that tell the same story as the essays.
