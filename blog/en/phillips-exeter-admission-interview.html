The Phillips Exeter interview

The central quad of the Exeter campus. Photo Transmarinus, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

US boarding schools November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

Every applicant to Exeter is interviewed, whatever the grade, the country or the strength of the rest of the file. Exeter wants that conversation to show what the child is passionate about, their favourite activity, the last book they read, anything that reveals what they love to do. It is a short list, and it is harder than it looks.

I have students at Exeter and prepare candidates for this interview every year. Here is how it works and what makes the difference.

One appointment, before January 15

Each applicant is entitled to a single interview, booked in the Exeter applicant portal once the Candidate Profile has been submitted on Gateway, and completed by January 15. There is no second attempt. A child who has a bad morning, a cold or a poor connection on that day lives with it, which is a good reason not to place the interview in the last week before the deadline.

Three formats, three different exercises

FormatWhat it involvesWhat it suits
On campusA conversation in New Hampshire, usually combined with a tourFamilies able to travel; children who are at ease face to face
OnlineA live conversation by video callFamilies abroad; children comfortable on screen
Recorded videoAnswers filmed in response to questions supplied by the schoolTime zones or schedules that make a live slot impossible

The recorded video looks like the easy option and is often the hardest. There is no one opposite to smile, no follow-up question to rescue an answer that started badly, and a child speaking to a camera tends either to recite or to freeze. The live formats are conversations; the recorded one is a performance, and it should be chosen knowingly.

Ready for the conversation

The Exeter interview cannot be learned by heart, but it can be prepared. I work with the child, in English and in the chosen format, until they talk about what they love with ease and substance.

Book a conversation

What the conversation is really about

The questions are open on purpose. "What do you love to do?" has no right answer, and that is the test: whether the child has something to say once the obvious answer is out. Saying that one plays the cello is information. Explaining which piece is giving trouble at the moment, and why, is a conversation.

The same goes for the last book. Almost every child names one. Fewer can say what they thought of it, what they disagreed with, or what it led them to read next. The interviewer is not checking the reading list; they are listening for a mind at work.

Can your child talk about what they love, in English, to an adult they have never met? Tell me about them.

The Harkness table behind the interview

Every class at Exeter is taught around an oval table of no more than twelve students and a teacher, and lessons are discussions rather than lectures. A student who cannot think aloud, listen to someone else and build on what was said will struggle there, however strong their marks. The interview is the only part of the file where the school hears the child think in real time. It reads much like a seat at that table.

Interviewing in a second language

For a child educated in another language, the interview is also where English shows most clearly. Fluency is not the point; a child who searches for a word and then says something interesting does better than one who speaks smoothly and says little. The real risk is different: answers prepared in the home language and translated in the head come out stiff and short. Practice has to happen in English from the start.

Prepared, not rehearsed

Interviewers see many coached candidates, and a memorised answer is recognisable within a few sentences: too finished, too general, and unable to survive a follow-up. Good preparation does the opposite. It helps the child find the two or three subjects they can talk about for ten minutes, practise being interrupted, and get used to saying "I don't know, but I think..." without panic.

How I prepare candidates for the Exeter interview

I have students at Phillips Exeter and for seven years have worked with around fifteen families a year aiming for the most selective schools in the United States, England and Switzerland. For the interview, I work with the child in short sessions, in English and in the format the family has chosen, on real conversations rather than scripts. The aim is a child who arrives knowing what they want to share and able to follow the conversation wherever it goes.
