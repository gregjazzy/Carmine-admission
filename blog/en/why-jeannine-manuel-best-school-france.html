Why Ecole Jeannine Manuel Is One of France's Best Schools

Photo: École Jeannine Manuel

Elite Schools February 2026 · updated November 2026 5 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. Has worked with students of École Jeannine Manuel for more than ten years.

Book a conversation

I am often asked whether École Jeannine Manuel deserves its reputation. For more than ten years, I have worked with students of the school, from their admission all the way to the university or grande école they are aiming for, and I see up close what it gives them. Here is what really sets it apart, and which children it is made for.

A bilingual school since 1954

The school opened in September 1954, as the École Active Bilingue, in a private mansion on avenue de La Bourdonnais. Its founder, Jeannine Manuel, started from one conviction: fear born of ignorance is at the root of conflict between nations and cultures. The school later moved to 70 rue du Théâtre, in Paris's 15th arrondissement, and has kept the same motto ever since: "International understanding through a bilingual education".

Bilingualism is not an option there. Teachers teach in their mother tongue, and the student body is one third French, one third dual nationals and one third non-French speakers. The school is under contract with the French state: it follows the official national curriculum, except in English, science and Chinese, where it has its own.

Two tracks in lycée: the BFI or the IB

In the last two years of lycée, students choose between two programmes. The French International Baccalaureate (BFI), in the American section, keeps the French curriculum and adds Advanced Placement courses recognised by American universities. The International Baccalaureate (IB) is an international diploma, taught mostly in English, and considerably more expensive. I compare the two tracks, and their cost, in admission in sixième, quatrième or seconde and in what the BFI changes after graduation.

Strong outcomes abroad

The school prepares its students for higher education early: from troisième (age 14), they attend presentations on universities and on the countries where they might study. It states that it sends more students to Oxford and Cambridge, and to Ivy League universities, than any other lycée in France.

I support some of its students all the way to those applications, and what I see confirms its reputation: the school knows how to present its students to universities abroad. The destinations are detailed in university outcomes after Jeannine Manuel.

Demanding mathematics

The French mathematics curriculum asks students to prove, not just to calculate. At Jeannine Manuel, that standard is held all the way to terminale, and the best students reach a level that opens the doors of French scientific prépas, engineering schools and science programmes at universities abroad. I see it every week with the top students I work with.

For a child coming from an English-speaking system, this is often where the gap is widest: they calculate well, but have never written a French-style proof.

Is your child coming from an English-speaking system? I measure their level with three tests at the school's level, in French, English and mathematics. Ask for an assessment.

Three campuses

In Paris, the school has three sites in the 15th arrondissement: Suffren and Dupleix for the youngest children, and rue du Théâtre from CM1 (age 9) to the final year. It opened a campus in Marcq-en-Barœul, near Lille, in 1992, with a boarding house that takes students from all over the world, and a school in London in 2015.

Which children it suits

Jeannine Manuel is not the best school for everyone. It is for a child ready to work in two languages, academically solid, curious, and aiming for the most selective programmes, in France or abroad. For a child who has grown up across several countries, it has another advantage: with one third of its students non-French speakers, their background is the norm there, not the exception.

A school in high demand

That reputation has a flip side: every year, I see most applicants leave without a place. What separates them happens in assessments and an interview whose content the school does not describe. I know it, because I have spent more than ten years working every week with its students and I prepare families for this admission.

With a family, I always start from a measurement: where the child stands in French, English and mathematics compared with the school's level, and how they come across in conversation. Everything else follows from that: the year to aim for, preparation for the tests and the interview, the application file, and backup schools in case Jeannine Manuel is not enough.
