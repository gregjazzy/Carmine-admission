Oxford and Cambridge French baccalaureate requirements: grades, colleges, interview

International Admissions October 2026 10 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

Cambridge publishes its admissions figures by country of residence. In the 2025 cycle it received 153 applications from students living in France, made 14 offers and admitted 12: a success rate of 7.8%, against 16.3% across all applicants. My reading is simple. Many French applicants send a file shaped for Parcoursup, the French national admissions platform, or for American colleges, and Oxford and Cambridge read files in a different way.

The admissions tests have their own pages on this site, starting with Oxford and Cambridge admissions tests. This article covers the door itself: the baccalaureate grades each university asks for, what the college choice really changes, the December interview, and how much selectivity varies by subject.

What average do Oxford and Cambridge want?

French grades run out of 20, and a 16 is a very strong result: it is the threshold of the mention très bien, the top honours band. The two universities convert their A level requirements into this scale differently. Oxford uses a sliding scale with half-point steps. Cambridge sets one floor and applies it to individual subjects as well.

The BFI, or Baccalauréat Français International, is the bilingual version of the French bac taken in international sections. Oxford gives it a one or two point discount; Cambridge does not. Cambridge also notes that in recent rounds most successful applicants exceeded 17, especially in relevant subjects. A bare 17 makes a file eligible, and the rest of the file has to make it competitive.

The phrase "17 in individual subjects" deserves a close look. Cambridge's central page says individual subjects; Trinity's guidance says relevant ones. For a student whose philosophy mark or an early written French exam pulls the profile down, the wording matters, and it should be clarified in writing with the college before the application goes in, not in July. In mathematics, Cambridge offers almost always add STEP, covered in STEP from a French lycée.

Predicted grades and the weight of the past

The application goes in during October of terminale, the final year. Universities therefore decide on predicted grades, which the referee enters for every exam still to be sat. I explain how to prepare a French school for that in UCAS with the French baccalaureate; two points are specific to Oxford and Cambridge.

The school record weighs heavily. Oxford's PPE department publishes the weighting it uses before interviews: the admissions test, the GCSE "or equivalent" profile and predicted results are rated high, the teacher's reference medium, the personal statement low. For a French student, the GCSE equivalent is the report cards of seconde and première, the two years before the final one. A middling seconde shows.

Not every French school is registered with UCAS. On The Student Room, applicants from French lycées describe applying as individual candidates, with a teacher who uploads the reference through the link UCAS sends. It works, provided that teacher writes good English and understands what a grade prediction commits them to.

The college question

You apply to the university and are admitted by a college. Families often spend weeks on this choice. The published figures suggest spending less.

At Oxford, an open application, made without naming a college, is allocated automatically to a college with fewer direct applicants for that subject. Oversubscribed colleges must also pass some shortlisted candidates on. In computer science, 28.2% of 2025 offers were open offers or came from a college other than the first one considering the applicant. In PPE, 127 shortlisted applicants were reallocated.

Cambridge calls its version the winter pool. Around 18% of applications received in October 2025 were pooled, and in January 2026, 1,060 pooled applicants, roughly one in four, were offered a place by a college they had not applied to.

So I suggest choosing a college for reasons of life rather than strategy. One practical point is worth checking at Cambridge, though: interview format depends on the college. King's and Churchill interview every candidate in person, including those living abroad, while Sidney Sussex, Trinity Hall and Christ's interview overseas applicants online. For a student in Singapore or Dubai, a trip to Cambridge in early December, in the middle of the first term of the final year, is a real cost.

The December interview

Oxford describes it as an academic conversation about the subject, close to a tutorial, usually led by two tutors. Its interviews are online, in early to mid December, and cannot be rearranged. A mathematics candidate has one or two sessions of about 25 minutes at the first college and at least one at another.

Oxford's PPE document goes further than most official pages. Each interview is scored out of 100: above 70 is a strong indicator for admission, and most admitted candidates score above 65. Tutors look for understanding, shown by listening and restating someone else's idea in your own words; intellectual flexibility, meaning the readiness to take an objection on board; critical reasoning; and clear communication. Cambridge warns French bac applicants that interviews in computer science, economics and natural sciences will probably include mathematics questions.

Accounts from applicants on The Student Room are strikingly consistent. Many come out convinced they failed and still receive an offer. Offer holders repeat that tutors give hints along the way and do not expect candidates to reach every answer alone, and that thinking out loud is what saved them.

Where French schooling leaves a gap

To my mind this is the deepest difference between the two systems. A French lycée trains students to hand in a finished, fully written answer and to deliver a prepared oral, including the grand oral at the end of the bac. It almost never trains them to work through a problem in front of someone. Faced with an unfamiliar question, a strong French student goes quiet, works in their head, and speaks only once they have the answer. The Oxford tutor sees silence. Oxford's Mathematical Institute puts it plainly: if you say nothing, they cannot tell what you are thinking.

The second habit to unlearn is defensiveness. A student used to having work marked hears an objection as a correction. In the interview, an objection is an invitation to adjust and carry on. The candidate who digs in loses ground on flexibility, even if the first idea was right.

This can be trained, though not in a week. Useful practice looks like the real thing: working aloud, in English, through problems never seen before, with someone who interrupts the way a tutor would, offers a hint halfway and asks why an idea was dropped. No student enjoys it at first. The point is to learn that being wrong in front of someone is survivable, and that is what changes the interview most.

Acceptance rates by subject

Between computer science and languages the ratio is roughly one to seven. Oxford publishes applicants per place instead, averaged over three years (2023 to 2025): 18.8 in Economics and Management, 14.8 in Computer Science, 10.4 in Mathematics. In the 2025 cycle alone it received 23,329 applications and made 3,891 offers. In PPE, 681 of 1,885 applicants were interviewed and 261 received an offer.

I never advise choosing a subject for its odds. A file built around a subject the student does not love falls apart at interview. But a bilingual student genuinely torn between economics and a languages and history degree should know the two doors are not the same width.

Example: the cycle for entry in 2027.

UCAS application for Oxford and Cambridge by 15 October 2026, 6 pm UK time. Admissions tests from 12 to 16 October 2026. Oxford interviews online in early and mid December; at Cambridge, 30 November to 16 December 2026 at Christ's, 4 to 18 December at Trinity Hall, the week of 7 December at King's. Oxford decisions on 12 January 2027.

Fees for students paying the international rate, 2027 entry: at Cambridge, £30,798 for humanities, law and economics, £34,356 for mathematics and £46,872 for sciences and engineering, £70,554 for medicine, plus a college fee that varies by college. Oxford publishes its fees course by course on its Course fees page. A French student does not always pay the international rate: the cases where home fees apply, and the case of a family that moved to London after 2021, are in Study in the UK with a French baccalaureate.

A timeline that works

For a student applying in the autumn of the final year, most of the file is decided earlier than families expect. The seconde and première reports are already written. The admissions test is prepared over the preceding summer, and so is the personal statement, which only works when the student writes about the subject rather than about themselves; the method is in the UCAS personal statement for French students. Interview practice starts in October at the latest, straight after the test, and is better started before.
