Le Rosey: the entrance exam and the day on campus

The Château du Rosey, Rolle. Photo Eric Bajart, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Boarding schools November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

At Le Rosey, the entrance exam does not happen in an anonymous hall: it is part of a whole day on the Rolle campus, where the child discovers the school while the school discovers the child. Fifteen of my students have been admitted to Le Rosey, and I have helped others into Aiglon and Rosenberg. What preparing them has taught me is that this day is rarely won on the written papers alone.

One day, three papers and an interview

Once the online file is complete, the child is invited to spend a day at Le Rosey, no earlier than fifteen months before the intended start. They sit tests in mathematics, English or French, and their mother tongue, followed by an interview with an academic advisor, who also answers their questions. The procedure is the same at every age, from the youngest to candidates for the upper classes.

The papers are marked by Le Rosey teachers. The file is then read in detail by three people, including the Headmaster, and discussed at the weekly admissions meeting with the Director General, the Headmaster and the Director of Admissions. Every paper and every exchange of the day passes before the people who decide.

The mother-tongue paper, the one families forget

Families prepare maths and the working language. Almost none think about the mother tongue. Yet a Russian, Chinese or Brazilian child schooled in English for years often writes their own language less well than anyone assumes. That paper sits on the programme of the day just like mathematics, and a weak script in the child's own language gives a worrying picture of an otherwise brilliant candidate.

Does your child still write their mother tongue well? Tell me about their schooling: it is something I always check.

The level the school expects

Le Rosey requires every candidate to be capable, in due course, of following the programmes of the French Baccalaureate or the International Baccalaureate. On languages, the youngest, up to the age of twelve, can arrive without French or English; from twelve, they need to understand one of the school's two languages; from fifteen, they must master one. The older the child, the heavier the language paper weighs, and the more a gap costs.

What the interview is looking for

Le Rosey favours active, curious and ambitious candidates, and wants the child and the family to share the values of its codes. It also makes clear whom it will not take: a child with violent or contemptuous attitudes, or a spoiled child used to always getting their way, because life and discipline at Le Rosey are demanding. That is what the interview is for. A candidate who comes across as polite but passive, or who talks about the school as an entitlement, does themselves far more harm than an average maths mark.

Preparing for the day, not just the papers

A well-prepared child arrives in Rolle knowing how the day unfolds, having worked through papers at Le Rosey's level, in the school's language and in their mother tongue, and able to talk about themselves, what drives them and what they expect from boarding life. They have not learned answers. They are simply ready to show who they are, at their best.
