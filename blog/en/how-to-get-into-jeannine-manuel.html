How to Get Into Jeannine Manuel: The Complete Admission Guide

EJM Admission January 2026 8 min read

Ecole Jeannine Manuel is one of the most sought-after schools in France. Every year, thousands of families submit applications. The vast majority are rejected. If you are reading this article, you are probably considering an application for your child and want to understand what actually makes the difference. Here is what the brochures do not tell you.

After having guided more than 1,600 students through admissions processes, including several hundred targeting EJM, I am sharing here an unfiltered view of the process. This guide covers selection criteria, the admissions calendar, entrance tests, the interview, the application file, and the mistakes that cost families an admission.

What EJM actually looks for

Before discussing preparation, you need to understand what the school evaluates. EJM does not simply select the best students on academic grounds. It builds a community around a specific set of criteria:

  • Academic level: solid and consistent results, particularly in mathematics, French, and English. EJM looks for consistency, not an isolated spike in performance.
  • French-English bilingualism: this is a structural prerequisite, not a bonus. The school evaluates true proficiency in both languages, written and spoken. Asymmetric bilingualism (excellent English but fragile French, or the reverse) is a red flag.
  • Personality and intellectual curiosity: EJM is interested in students who think, question, and engage. The profile of the quiet high achiever who checks every box but never raises a hand is not the one that stands out.
  • Adaptability: a child who has lived in multiple countries, changed school systems, and had to integrate into different environments possesses a competency that EJM explicitly values.

The admissions calendar: every week counts

The admissions process at EJM follows a rigid calendar. Failing to know it means falling behind before you start. Here are the key milestones:

  • October-November (Year N-1): online registration opens for the following September intake. This is the moment to submit the complete application. Families who wait until January find themselves at the back of the queue, or past the deadline entirely.
  • December-January: invitations to entrance tests. Dates are set by the school, with no negotiation possible. If you are abroad, you need to organize the trip.
  • February-March: interviews with candidates who passed the tests. This is the decisive phase.
  • March-April: admission decisions communicated to families.
  • April-May: waitlist management. Spots can open up, but building a strategy on this hypothesis is a mistake.

The golden rule: begin preparing the application at least 12 months before the target start date. If you are aiming for a September 2027 entry, your preparation must begin by summer 2026 at the latest.

The entrance tests: where selection actually happens

EJM's tests are not simple diagnostic assessments. They are discriminating examinations, designed to differentiate among candidates whose files are already strong. Three subjects are evaluated:

French

The French test covers comprehension, written expression, and language mastery. The expected level corresponds to the French national curriculum. A student who has been educated in an anglophone system for several years, even if they speak French at home, can find themselves in difficulty with essay writing, text analysis, or advanced grammar exercises. Conversational French is not sufficient. Academic French, the ability to construct an argument in writing, use precise vocabulary, and demonstrate syntactical sophistication, is what the test evaluates.

English

The English test assesses the student's ability to function in an English-language academic environment. For candidates coming from francophone systems, this is often the weak point. EJM expects operational proficiency, not school-level English. The test involves literary analysis, structured essay writing, and comprehension of sophisticated texts. Students who have studied English as a foreign language in a French school will typically find this section challenging.

Mathematics

This is the test that produces the most surprises. EJM's mathematics tests follow the French national program. And curricular progressions differ considerably from one system to another. A student in 10th grade in the American system or in Year 11 of the British system has not necessarily covered the same material as a French student in Seconde, particularly in algebra, geometry, and formal proofs. The IB system (MYP) presents the same gap. Families who do not specifically prepare for this test discover it on the day of the exam, when it is too late.

The interview: revealing a personality

Candidates who pass the tests are invited to an interview. It is conducted in both French and English, and covers the student's background, motivations, interests, and vision for the future.

EJM is not looking for scripted answers. It is looking for authenticity, self-awareness, and the ability to speak about oneself with a minimum of depth. A student who recites a list of extracurricular activities without being able to explain what they gained from them will not make an impression. Conversely, a student who can articulate how a move across the world transformed them, or how a particular book changed their perspective on a subject, demonstrates exactly the kind of maturity the school seeks.

The interview is also an opportunity for EJM to evaluate the candidate's real linguistic fluency. A natural switch from French to English, without hesitation or loss of precision, sends a powerful signal. Preparation for the interview must therefore include practice in both languages, with structured responses that feel natural rather than rehearsed.

The application file: every element counts

School transcripts

EJM requests transcripts from the last three years. If they are in another language, a French translation is required. The school analyzes the trajectory: a steady progression is valued; erratic results raise questions. If a dip is explained by a change of country or system, it must be contextualized in the file. An unexplained drop in grades will be interpreted negatively.

The motivation letter

For lycee-level entries, the student writes a motivation letter. This is a delicate exercise. The letter must be authentic, personal, and specific to EJM. Generic letters that could apply to any school are immediately spotted. The student must explain why this school, not why a good school. Specificity demonstrates that the family has done their homework and understands the institution's values and culture.

Extracurricular activities

EJM is interested in what the student does outside the classroom. Not to accumulate lines on a CV, but to understand what drives them. Community involvement, sustained artistic practice, a personal project: these are signals of curiosity and initiative. Depth matters more than quantity. A student who has spent three years deeply committed to one activity is far more compelling than one who has sampled twelve.

The profile that makes the difference

After years of guiding families toward EJM, one observation is inescapable: the students who get admitted are not simply those with the best grades. They are those who embody a specific profile:

  • An international mindset: not just a passport or a resume of countries lived in, but a way of thinking that naturally integrates the diversity of perspectives.
  • Visible intellectual curiosity: the student who reads beyond the curriculum, who takes interest in subjects outside their academic framework, who asks questions nobody else asks.
  • A capacity for self-reflection: the ability to speak about strengths and limitations with lucidity. EJM trains students who think, not students who execute.

The numbers: brutal selectivity

The reality is brutal: the admission rate for external candidates coming from abroad structurally plateaus between 10% and 15%. This means that out of ten solid applications submitted by expatriate families, eight or nine will be rejected. The spots are rare, the competition fierce, and a good file is not enough: you need an exceptional file.

It is explained by surgical preparation: precise assessment of the student's profile, targeted remediation, specific test preparation, interview coaching, and construction of an application that tells a coherent story. The mistakes that cost an admission

Applying too late

This is the number one mistake. EJM's calendar is rigid. An application submitted in February for a September entry has virtually zero chance of succeeding. Families who are still "thinking about it" in January have already lost several critical weeks.

Underestimating the French level

A child who has grown up abroad since primary school and speaks French at home is often two years behind in academic French compared to the French curriculum. EJM detects this immediately. The level must be assessed honestly and, if necessary, remediation must begin well before the tests.

Not preparing for the tests

The tests are not a formality. They are designed to eliminate. A brilliant student who is unprepared for the French format of mathematics examinations will be penalized. This is not a question of ability. It is a question of preparation.

Not having a Plan B

EJM rejects more candidates than it accepts. Families without an alternative find themselves in a critical situation in April. You must systematically apply in parallel to two or three other schools: the Lycee International de Saint-Germain-en-Laye, Franklin, or other lycees with international sections.

Neglecting the post-graduation dimension

Getting into EJM is not an end in itself. It is a lever toward the best post-secondary formations. Families who are not already thinking about university outcomes after Jeannine Manuel risk not fully exploiting the potential of this institution.
