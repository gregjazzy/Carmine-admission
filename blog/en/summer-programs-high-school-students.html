Summer programs for high school students: which ones actually count in an application

International Admissions October 2026 9 min read

Every winter a parent shows me a glossy brochure. A famous university crest, two weeks "on campus", classes taught by faculty, and a bill north of six thousand dollars. Then the question: will this help with Harvard, or MIT, or Oxford?

I answer with a distinction that admissions readers make in a second and families rarely make at all. "Summer program" covers two products. One picks its students on problem sets, letters and grades, and costs little or nothing for those who need help. The other takes nearly anyone who can pay. The host university may be the same, and the admissions reader tells them apart at a glance.

One label, two markets

MIT says so plainly on its summer page. Most programs admit all or most students who can pay the often high tuition; a smaller group selects on merit and is free or comparatively affordable. The same page adds that there is no right or wrong way to spend a summer. Families tend to read past that second sentence.

What universities say about their own summer offerings

Universities that sell summer courses to teenagers are careful on one point: none promises an admissions edge. Harvard says its Pre-College Program, two weeks and non-credit, does not guarantee admission to the College, and that doing well in summer school strengthens an application "to any college or university". Stanford says admission to its pre-collegiate programs is kept separate from undergraduate admission, and that the value lies in what students learn there.

The British side is blunter. Cambridge's summer school FAQ notes that admissions tutors will not know a candidate attended unless the student mentions it in the personal statement. Its page on official programmes warns that a summer school held in a college may not be a Cambridge programme at all, since colleges rent rooms to outside organisers; the University recognises only two, one of them the Sutton Trust Summer School, which is limited to UK state-school pupils. Oxford's student newspaper ran a long piece on the private schools that take over college buildings each summer, and the University told it that these programmes are entirely separate from admissions.

An MIT admissions officer put it this way on the official blog in 2023: he cannot say participation will smooth the path to admission, and the committee looks hardest at individual initiative, and even more at the reasons behind it. Former admissions officers quoted in the American press go further and describe pre-college programs as a way to fill empty dorms in July. I would not use the phrase myself, but it explains why a crest on a certificate is not enough.

Two absences are worth a sentence. The Telluride Association's free humanities seminars carried the same weight with readers; the association has announced it will not run them in 2027 while it reviews the program. And PROMYS in Boston takes international students but says space is limited and aid may not be available to them. For a student living in France, the Oxford version is the right door.

The French exam calendar that American programs ignore

This is the point I see least anticipated. The most selective American programs target the summer before the final year of high school, which for a French student is the summer between première and terminale. That is exactly when France holds its early baccalaureate exams. The French written paper comes in mid-June, the new early maths exam (first sat in 2026) a week later, and the French oral can be scheduled into the second week of July. RSI, Ross, SSP and Mathcamp all start between mid-June and late June.

So a première student at a school in France usually cannot leave for RSI or Ross that summer without a special arrangement. French lycées abroad have their own exam calendar, published separately, and it has to be checked centre by centre. The programs that fit the French year start in mid-July or later: PROMYS Europe, the second or third Harvard Pre-College session, the Animath camp in August.

Example: summer 2027 for a première student in France

Baccalaureate (official calendar published 25 August 2026): French written exam 15 June, early maths exam 21 June, French oral between 21 June and 9 July, early exam results by 9 July.

Programs: RSI at MIT 20 June to 31 July, at Stanford 27 June to 7 August, applications closed 11 December 2026. SSP from mid-June, international deadline announced for January 2027. PROMYS Europe 11 July to 21 August, applications open in January (the 2026 round closed on 8 March). Harvard Pre-College sessions 21 June to 2 July, 12 to 23 July and 26 July to 6 August, priority deadline 13 January, regular deadline 10 February 2027.

Ross and Mathcamp post their 2027 dates in winter; in 2026 Ross ran 14 June to 24 July and Mathcamp 28 June to 2 August.

Deadlines fall in the winter of première, sometimes as early as December, so a student who hears about RSI in February has already missed the year. The PROMYS Europe, Ross and Mathcamp problems take weeks of work and must be written up in English as full proofs. French olympiad training prepares for them far better than any summer course does; I explain why the work matters more than the medal in Maths olympiads in a university application, and these applications follow the same logic.

So what about the paid programs?

I don't rule them out. Two weeks in Cambridge, Massachusetts can tell a fifteen-year-old that they really do want a US degree, or that they don't, and that is worth money. What fails is presenting them as an honour. A reader knows a pre-college program is paid for; seeing it at the top of the activities list, they mostly conclude the student had nothing stronger to show.

What works is what follows. A student who comes back from a summer economics course and builds, that autumn, a small study of rents in their neighbourhood from public data has a story. A student with a certificate of attendance does not. Holistic review, which I explain in Holistic admission explained to a French family, reads activities as a thread, and one isolated summer with nothing before or after is not a thread.

For UK applications it is simpler still. The third question of the UCAS personal statement asks what the student has done outside school to prepare for the subject. A summer program belongs there if it made the student work on that subject, and only to that extent.

What I would advise, depending on the student

For a strong maths student in seconde (Year 11) or première (Year 12), I start with the spring Coupe Animath and the August camp, then PROMYS Europe the summer after. Both fit the French calendar, cost little or come with aid, and train exactly what the Oxford, Cambridge and MIT selection tests later check.

For a science student aiming at research who has the scores, RSI is worth an email to CEE well before the autumn of première, to find out whether France is open that year, and a frank talk with the school about the early exams. For a humanities student, Telluride's pause in 2027 leaves few free programs at that level; a personal project carried through the summer (an investigation, a published piece, a work placement) will often weigh more than a paid seminar. And for a family weighing a six-thousand-dollar pre-college course, I ask what the student will do in September with what they learned in July. The timing mistakes I see most often among French families aiming at the Ivy League are set out in Getting into the Ivy League from France.
