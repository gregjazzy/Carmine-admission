Which School to Choose in Dubai: LFIGP, LLFP, DIA, GEMS...

Expat guide March 2026 12 min read

Moving to Dubai is about more than finding a villa in Jumeirah or a flat in the Marina. You also need to understand how each school sorts its applications, and what lies behind fees that can double from one school to the next for the same year group. The four schools below are the ones French families most often hesitate between.

1. The Rigorous Choice: accredited French schools

Lycée Français International Georges Pompidou (LFIGP)

LFIGP has around 2,600 pupils on two sites: Oud Metha, in the heart of the city, for nursery, and Academic City for primary, middle and high school. It is an AEFE partner school, meaning a private school accredited by the French Ministry of Education, not a school run by the Agency itself. It offers the BFI (the international option of the French baccalauréat) in the last two years, in a bilingual or trilingual version.

Baccalauréat pass rate 2026 100%

Last three years AED 50,700 a year

The detail that matters

LFIGP ranks applications in order of arrival, by date and time, within priority categories. A child coming from a French school goes ahead of one coming from another curriculum, even a French child. In the year groups in demand, applying on opening day or three weeks later makes a real difference.

A word on timing. At LFIGP, applications for the following school year open in the autumn, on a day and at a time announced in advance. I have seen families arrive in March for a September start: in the year groups in demand, the places had gone long before. The priorities and fees of each French school, AFLEC and Jean-Mermoz included, are set out in our article on the French schools in Dubai.

Lycée Libanais Francophone Privé (LLFP) · Meydan

LLFP Meydan, in the Nad Al Sheba district, teaches the French curriculum from the youngest nursery class to the final year, and it is accredited by the AEFE from Petite Section (age 3) to Terminale. Less well known than Pompidou, it draws a varied French-speaking community: Lebanese, French, African.

2. Global Immersion: The IB Programme

Dubai International Academy (DIA) · Emirates Hills

DIA, in Emirates Hills, belongs to the Innoventures Education group. It offers the IB programmes: PYP in primary, MYP in middle school and the Diploma Programme in the last two years. For many international families settled in "new Dubai", it is the reference IB school.

IB World School

DIA Emirates Hills

KG1 AED 44,979 a year
Diploma years AED 79,696 a year
Entrance test AED 525

The extra lines

A new pupil pays 10% of the annual fees on enrolment, non-refundable and deducted from the first term, plus AED 4,500 a year if English language support (EAL) is needed. For a child coming from a French school without solid English, that is a line to budget for.

GEMS World Academy (GWA)

GEMS World Academy, in Al Barsha South, is the high-end IB school of the GEMS Education group. Its fees are among the highest in Dubai: over AED 100,000 a year from Grade 6 onwards.

Ask for it in writing

The school states that its fees exclude uniforms, food, transport, and English or special needs support. Before comparing with a French school, those are the lines to put a figure on.

3 traps families discover too late

  1. Hidden costs. Published tuition is only the base. At LFIGP, the school bus costs AED 8,113 to 8,527 a year, both ways, for a high school pupil. At DIA, English language support (EAL) costs AED 4,500 a year on top. Uniforms, food, trips and English support come on top everywhere. Always ask for the total cost of attendance.
  2. Silent waiting lists. Some schools accept your application without telling you that you are on a waiting list. You get a polite "we'll be in touch" in February, then nothing until June. Meanwhile, places at other schools fill up, and you no longer have a plan B.
  3. The "we'll see later" trap. Many families choose a school for primary, telling themselves they will adjust for middle school. But at LFIGP, a child coming from another curriculum goes behind those coming from a French school, and after a few years in the British or IB system, their written French is often no longer at the level of Sixième (Year 7). The transition that looked simple becomes a wall.

This is the kind of situation we anticipate in our strategic guidance process.

What we see on the ground

A family reached out to us in January. Their 14-year-old son was arriving from Singapore. Waitlisted at LFI GP, rejected by DIA : application filed too late. We prepared an urgent application for LLFP and secured a fast-tracked interview. He started in September.

Another case we see often: the father wants IB "because it's international," the mother wants the French track "just in case we go back." Their daughter is in Grade 8, they're planning three years in Dubai. We ask the one question that matters: "Are you returning to France afterwards?" Yes, probably. The BFI at Pompidou becomes the obvious choice. Two years later, she's in Grade 10 BFI with a 16/20 average. The same choice between the French network and an international school comes up in every expat city, and the criteria that settle it are in my guide to French schools abroad.

BFI vs IB: don't make the wrong choice

This is where most families make a critical mistake. The common assumption: "My child wants to study internationally, so they need the IB." This is wrong.

What parents don't always see: the choice made in Grade 6 in Dubai determines the options available in the final year, and therefore which universities are within reach. You're not choosing a school for the next 3 years. You're laying the foundation of an application for Stanford, EPFL, or Sciences Po in 5 or 6 years. We break down this impact in our full BFI vs IB analysis for university admissions.

The BFI (Baccalauréat Français International) adds an advanced foreign language and literature to the French baccalauréat, and universities abroad read it as a demanding programme. But it's not the right option for everyone either : it depends on your situation:

  • The length of your expatriation : 2 years and 6 years are not the same strategy
  • Post-graduation plans : and these often shift along the way
  • Maths, English and written French level : this is often where difficulties emerge
  • Age at the time of the switch : a transition in Grade 6 and in Grade 10 carry very different consequences
  • Likelihood of returning to France : the factor families realise too late (see our guide on orientation mistakes when returning from abroad)
