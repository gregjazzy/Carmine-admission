Eton Stage 2: the interview and the online test

A boy who clears Stage 1 is invited to Eton in the spring term of Year 6. He comes to the school with other boys of roughly his age and goes through two things: an individual interview and an online test. A few weeks later, his family learns whether he has a conditional place, a place on the waiting list, or nothing.

Of all the steps between registration and the first day in F Block, this is the one that decides most. It is also the one families prepare least well, often because they prepare the wrong thing.

Two exercises, two different questions

The online test at Stage 2 is not a harder version of the Pre-Tests. It is designed to predict academic ability and potential, rather than to measure what a boy has already been taught. A boy who has spent months on past papers of other tests gains little from them here.

The interview is a conversation with an adult he has never met. Eton is weighing two things at once: how able the boy is now and how far he may go, and whether he will be happy and contribute in a boarding house of fifty-five boys. Sport, music, drama and the rest are not separate criteria, but they come through strongly, along with what the school calls all-round personality.

The interview, prepared in depth

At eleven, the difference between two able boys often shows in a single conversation. I prepare candidates for that conversation so that the boy in the room is the one his teachers know, thinking clearly and speaking for himself.

What an interviewer hears

An eleven-year-old cannot be judged on knowledge alone; he has not lived long enough to know much. What an interviewer can hear is how he thinks. Does he listen to the whole question before answering? When he meets something he does not know, does he stop, guess, or reason his way towards an answer and say so? Can he talk about a book, a hobby or a match in a way that shows he has actually thought about it?

Boys who do well are rarely the ones with the most polished answers. A boy who has rehearsed what to say about his favourite subject sounds like it within a minute, and the interviewer moves to ground where the rehearsal does not reach. Preparation that works builds the habit of thinking aloud, not a script.

Groups, tranches and the wait

Boys are seen in groups by age, and offers follow the same logic. Eton announces its decisions in three tranches, in March, May and July, according to when the boy's birthday falls in the school year. A boy born in September may know his outcome months before one born in June, and neither result says anything about the other. Families who need a bursary of 80% or more are told earlier, in February, once the bursaries committee has assessed their case.

The wait is long for an eleven-year-old who knows what is at stake. How it is handled at home matters more than most parents expect, particularly for the boys in the last tranche.

Three possible outcomes

The first is a conditional place, confirmed in Year 8 by the King's Scholarship, Common Entrance or Eton Entrance. The second is a waiting-list place: the boy returns in the summer of Year 7 for a second interview and sits the online test again, and Eton takes a number of these boys every year. The third is no place. For the second group especially, what happens in Year 7 is decisive, and a precise reading of why Stage 2 did not go further is the starting point.

How I prepare boys for Stage 2

I have students at Eton and each year work with around fifteen families aiming for the most selective schools, in England, Switzerland and the United States. For Stage 2, the preparation is about the boy himself: the way he reasons when he is not sure, the way he talks about what he loves, the confidence to hold a conversation with an adult who is assessing him. It is demanding work, done at the level of the boys he will be compared with, and it leaves no trace of coaching in the room.
