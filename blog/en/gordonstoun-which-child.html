Which child is Gordonstoun for?

Gordonstoun House, Moray, Scotland. Photo Nibaba, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

When Prince Philip arrived at Gordonstoun in 1934, aged 13, the school had just been founded by Kurt Hahn, a German educator who had fled the Nazis. He left five years later as Guardian, the head of the school, with a lifelong love of the sea, and later gave his name to the Duke of Edinburgh's Award, which grew out of a Gordonstoun scheme. Hahn's idea was blunt: it is "culpable neglect" not to push the young into experiences. Ninety years on, the school still works that way, and it suits some children far better than others.

The child who comes alive outdoors

Every Senior pupil sails, from dinghies to the school's 80 ft ketch, and goes on expeditions in the hills of the north of Scotland. For a child who is restless in a classroom but focused on a boat or a hillside, this is not a distraction from learning; it is where confidence is built, and the confidence returns to the classroom.

The child who needs a responsibility

Pupils are given responsibility early: in the house, in a rescue or community service, on a crew. A child who grows when trusted with something real, and drifts when nothing depends on them, often finds the right pressure here.

The child who wants the world

A third of the pupils come from overseas, from more than forty countries, a third from Scotland and a third from the rest of the UK. Houses are mixed deliberately. Exchanges reach as far as Australia and Japan, and service projects go to Kenya, Romania and Thailand. A curious child, open to people unlike themselves, gains a great deal.

The capable student who is not yet stretched

The academic programme leads to GCSEs, A levels and BTECs, with weekly tutor meetings and subject clinics, and pupils go on to universities including Oxford and Cambridge. Pupils arriving in the Sixth Form with an average GCSE grade of 6 have gone on to 74% A and B grades at A level. That is the profile of a school that adds value to good students, rather than one that only admits the top of the cohort.

The young performer

Gordonstoun is the only school in Scotland where dance is part of the academic curriculum, and its drama department has toured to the Edinburgh Fringe and New York. A child gifted on stage keeps that gift alive alongside everything else.

When another school would be wiser

  • A child who needs quiet weekends and time alone may find a seven-day boarding week exhausting.
  • A family that wants an exam-only school, where the timetable is built around results first, will find the co-curriculum a constraint rather than the point.
  • A child who hates cold, wind and water should be listened to: sail training is for everyone.
  • A family that needs the child home most weekends will find the distance hard; leave-out weekends are usually two per term.
