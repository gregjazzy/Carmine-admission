How NCSSM selects

The NCSSM entrance in Durham, North Carolina. Photo Warren LeMay, Wikimedia Commons, CC0

NCSSM does not admit by test score or by a single admissions officer's judgement. Its applications are read by committees of experienced North Carolina educators, guidance counselors, retired teachers and other professionals, organised in teams of three. Each team reviews the applications from one congressional district and scores every candidate on a rubric. The maximum is 102 points for Residential candidates and 93 for Online candidates, who do not take the math assessment.

Only two years of grades count

For the academic record, NCSSM considers only courses completed in 9th and 10th grade. High school courses taken in middle school, in 7th or 8th grade, are not factored in. A student who took Math 1 early gains no points for it in this part of the rubric; what counts is how they handled the hardest courses available to them in the last two years.

"Need for NCSSM" is not about a weak school

Families often misread this criterion. It is not a question of whether the applicant can get a good education at their current school. It asks whether NCSSM offers something unique and essential that the current school, however good, cannot provide, and whether the student's curiosity has reached a point where they need that environment to reach their potential. A strong applicant from a strong school can score well here, if they can say precisely what they would do at NCSSM that they cannot do now.

Can your child explain why they need NCSSM? Let's work on it together.

Read by district

Because each team reviews one congressional district, applicants are read alongside others from their part of the state, by educators who know its schools. Graduates come from all thirteen districts.

What does not count

SAT and ACT scores are neither required nor accepted for the Class of 2029. Courses from middle school do not count toward rigour.
