The UTS group task

On January 23, 2027, alongside the interview stations, UTS places Stage 2 applicants in small groups for a group task. Five or six children who have never met are given something to do together. The rule that matters is stated plainly: applicants are not evaluated on whether the task succeeds, but on how they collaborate.

That rule changes everything about how a child should behave in the room, and it is the opposite of what many high-achieving children instinctively do. I prepare candidates for it every year.

What is being watched

The interview stations assess qualities such as teamwork, empathy, maturity and communication one child at a time. The group task shows the same qualities live, with other children. Observers see who listens, who builds on another child's idea, who notices that someone has not spoken, who proposes a way to organize the work, who can disagree without shutting a conversation down, and who keeps an eye on the time without taking over.

Because the outcome is not scored, a group that fails to finish can contain several children who did very well, and a group that finishes brilliantly can contain one who did badly because they finished it alone.

The three common mistakes

BehaviourWhy it costs
Taking charge to get the task doneThe task does not count; ignoring others' ideas does
Staying quiet to avoid mistakesObservers have nothing to assess
Agreeing with everythingNo contribution of one's own, no reasoning visible

The first is the most frequent among strong applicants. A child used to being the best in class sees the answer, organizes everyone around it and pushes the group to finish. It looks like leadership. In this format it reads as not listening.

What good collaboration looks like in practice

A child who does well usually makes a few simple moves. They speak early enough to be part of the conversation. They refer to what others said, by name if they remember it. They ask a quieter child what they think. They propose a structure when the group stalls, and accept a better one when it comes. They disagree with a reason, not with a verdict. None of this requires charisma; it requires attention to other people, which is exactly what UTS is looking for.

Preparing for a group of strangers

The difficulty is that a child cannot rehearse the real group. What can be practised is the behaviour: short collaborative exercises with other children or adults, followed by honest feedback on how much the child listened, spoke, included others and moved the group forward. A reserved child needs to practise speaking up early; a dominant one needs to practise asking before deciding. A few sessions make the difference visible.
