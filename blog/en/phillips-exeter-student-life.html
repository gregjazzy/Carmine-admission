Life at Phillips Exeter: Harkness, Dorms and the Real Workload

Most of what makes Exeter different fits on a piece of furniture. In 1930 the philanthropist Edward Harkness gave the school $5.8 million on one condition: classes would be taught around an oval table, with no more than twelve students and a teacher. The school still describes the table in feet, seven by eleven, and there is one in every classroom. Before deciding whether Exeter suits a child, it helps to picture four years at that table, then the rest of the day around it.

Harkness, in practice

There is no lecture in a Harkness class. Students prepare beforehand, and the hour is spent discussing, arguing, correcting each other, with the teacher steering more than explaining. In 1931 a teacher named Henry S. Couse wrote of "a freedom of discussion" he had never seen before. A student who has not done the reading has nowhere to hide: with twelve people around a table, silence is noticed.

Ask Exeter students and alumni what defines the school, and Harkness comes first. A few alumni add a nuance worth hearing: the method shines in history or literature and works less naturally in math. For a strong student used to lectures and worksheets, the content is rarely the problem. Learning to speak up, every day, in front of eleven confident peers, is the real adjustment.

The shape of the week

Classes run from 8 am to 6 pm on weekdays, with some Saturdays. The year is divided into three terms, students usually take five courses, and physical education and health are compulsory. Exeter offers no AP courses, arguing that its own courses often go further. Sixty percent of its 478 courses are taught at the level of a first-year college course or above, and the school counts ten foreign languages and ten off-campus programs.

Grades run from 11 (A) to 1 (D-), with E as a fail, and Exeter does not rank its students. One detail reassures parents who worry about the first months: all ninth-grade courses are graded pass or fail in the fall term. That cushion exists only for students who start in grade 9. A new student joining in grade 10 or 11 is graded from the first day.

The workload, as students describe it

When families compare Exeter with other top boarding schools, the workload is the first thing students bring up. Students put it at three to five hours of homework a night, often around three hours in ninth grade rising to four and a half by eleventh. Those are their estimates, not school data, but they are consistent with a class day that ends at 6 pm.

Some students describe real stress and fatigue, a few in very harsh terms. They are a minority: the large majority of students rate their experience highly, and the complaints that come up most are mundane. Those complaints: the food, a dining hall that closes at 7 pm, long New Hampshire winters, and a rural campus that some describe as a bubble.

Dorm life

About 80 percent of students and 70 percent of teachers live on campus. Exeter has 26 dorms and houses, where 66 teachers live with the students, and two of them are all-gender. Dorms mix the four years, and students usually keep the same one from year to year, which makes it the closest thing to a family the school offers. Day students are attached to a dorm too, so they are not on the edge of that life.

Around the dorms: two dining halls and a café known as the Grill, close to 200 clubs, more than 50 community service groups, and 22 sports. Seventy percent of students play at least one. The rivalry with Andover, founded by the same Phillips family, goes back to a first baseball game in 1878.

For international students

Exeter brings together students from 45 states and 38 countries. International students arrive for an orientation at the end of August, receive their I-20 visa instructions at the end of April, and can join an International Student Alliance that meets every two weeks. The school does not publish the share of international students.

Who thrives at Exeter

The students who do best are not necessarily those with the highest scores. They are the ones who enjoy a discussion, prepare without being told twice, and can live away from home at fourteen without losing their footing. A child who learns well alone but freezes in a group, or who needs close supervision to work, can still succeed, but the first year will be harder. That is the question to settle before applying.
