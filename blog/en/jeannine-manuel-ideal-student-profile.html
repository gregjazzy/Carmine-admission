Jeannine Manuel: The Specific Profile They're Looking For Beyond Academic Excellence

Photo: École Jeannine Manuel

Ecole Jeannine Manuel February 2026 · updated November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. Has worked with students of École Jeannine Manuel for more than ten years.

Book a conversation

Many parents come to our first meeting with the same certainty: "My child has excellent grades, they should get into Jeannine Manuel." It is a line of reasoning I often have to correct. Good grades are necessary, never sufficient: I have seen it for more than ten years with the school's students I work with, and every year with the children I prepare to get in.

What the school says it looks for

The school writes that it admits students who it feels can benefit from a curriculum enriched by bilingualism, and whose families share its educational project. Between two equivalent applications, it takes interests and talents outside the classroom into account. It says no more than that. The rest becomes clear when you see who gets in and who thrives there.

Four qualities I see in the students who thrive there

1. Moving between two ways of thinking

This is not a French school that adds some English. Students constantly move between two traditions: personal argument in the English-speaking style on one side, proof and structured reasoning in the French style on the other, sometimes on the same day. A child who shines in a single system is not necessarily at ease there.

2. High standards in every subject

There is no acceptable weak subject. A student who is very strong in maths but does the minimum in French, or the reverse, soon finds themselves struggling. It is less about grades than about an attitude to work.

3. Stamina

The workload is heavy, in two languages, and it grows over the years. A student who loses their footing as soon as things get harder suffers there. A record with one difficult year followed by a real recovery is more reassuring than one with no setbacks at all.

4. Genuine engagement

The student with top marks everywhere who is interested in nothing outside class is not the one who stands out. The one who is truly invested in an activity, a project or their reading, and can talk about it, has an advantage.

What "international profile" really means

Many parents believe that having lived abroad, holding a foreign passport or speaking English is enough to make an international profile. At Jeannine Manuel, a third of the students are non-French speakers and another third hold dual nationality: a background abroad is common, it does not set an application apart. What counts is what the child has made of it.

I have seen children come back from ten years abroad having drawn little from it, and children who had never left Paris show real openness, through their family, their reading, their curiosity. The application and the interview have to show that openness, not just the passport stamps.

The interview: where personality becomes visible

The assessments measure academic level. The interview shows how the child thinks, listens to a question and answers it. In my experience, this is often where the difference between two close applications is made. Preparing for it is covered in the article on the admission interview.

I prepare your child for this interview through mock interviews, in French and in English. Tell me about your child.

Bilingual versus academically bilingual

A child can speak English every day, with friends or watching series, without being able to write an argued text or analyse a literary text in English. Yet the school requires written English from CE2 (age 8), a solid level written and spoken in middle school, and near-native English in lycée. This is the gap parents underestimate most, together with the written French of children schooled abroad.

To measure their written French and English, not just their conversation, I give them tests at the school's level. Ask for an assessment.

The uncomfortable truth about fit

With the students I work with at the school, the pattern is clear. Those who thrive enjoy complexity, cope with uncertainty, can handle several things at once, and draw energy from the diversity of their class. Those who struggle are often excellent students in a traditional setting: they need stable rules and a predictable pace. At Jeannine Manuel, you move from a highly structured class in French to a debate in English in the same morning.

Some brilliant students simply do not fit there, and some students with solid but unremarkable records are a perfect match. Neither profile is better than the other, but only one suits this school.

What this means for your strategy

Before applying, ask yourself honestly: is your child comfortable when the rules change? Do they genuinely enjoy languages, or put up with them because they are useful? Can they work intensely over a long period? Do they pursue interests of their own accord?

If the answer is yes across the board, Jeannine Manuel is probably an excellent choice. If you hesitate on several points, have your child assessed before committing. Forcing a child into a mould that does not fit them, however prestigious, is the worst mistake for them. For the full procedure, see the guide to getting into Jeannine Manuel.
