Phillips Exeter Alumni and College Results: Reading the Numbers

The library designed by Louis Kahn, opened in 1972. Photo Gunnar Klack, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Many families look at Exeter as a step toward the most selective colleges, and the school's results support that view. The way it publishes them, though, calls for a careful reading. Exeter gives score ranges and lists of universities grouped in bands, never the number of students who enrolled at each. Read carefully, they tell a good part of the story, not all of it.

The figures Exeter publishes

For the class of 2026, about 312 seniors:

  • 99 percent of graduates enroll in a four-year college;
  • middle 50 percent of SAT scores: 1360 to 1530, with average section scores of 711 in reading and writing and 726 in math;
  • middle 50 percent of ACT scores: 31 to 35;
  • 82 percent of AP exam scores at 4 or 5.

That last figure deserves a moment. Exeter offers no AP courses at all. Its students sit AP exams without the courses designed to prepare for them, and four out of five scores still come out at 4 or 5. For the class of 2027, the school also counts 27 National Merit semifinalists and 70 commended students.

Where graduates enroll

For the classes of 2024 to 2026, Exeter sorts colleges into two groups. Those that received ten or more students over the three years include Harvard, Yale, Princeton, MIT, Columbia, Penn, Brown, Dartmouth, Cornell, Chicago, Georgetown, NYU, Michigan, UC Berkeley and Williams, 32 institutions in all. Those that received fewer than ten include Stanford, Caltech, Duke, Johns Hopkins, Northwestern, Rice, Vanderbilt, UCLA, Amherst and Pomona. Abroad, the list names Oxford, Imperial College, Edinburgh, St Andrews, McGill, Toronto, UBC, Trinity College Dublin, Bocconi and Sciences Po, as well as the dual degree between Columbia and Sciences Po. In all, Exeter graduates of those three years enrolled at 196 institutions.

How to read these bands

"Ten or more over three years" covers about 930 graduates. It can mean ten students, or a hundred. Nothing Exeter publishes allows anyone to write "Exeter sends so many students to Harvard each year", and figures of that kind found elsewhere do not come from Exeter.

The second band is more telling. Stanford received fewer than ten Exeter graduates over three years, which is fewer than four a year out of more than 300. Alumni themselves say admissions to the most selective colleges are not what they used to be, and the numbers bear that out. Being an Exeter student helps; it does not replace a strong college application, which each student still has to build.

For families outside the United States, the international line matters. Oxford, Imperial, St Andrews, McGill, Toronto, Bocconi, Sciences Po and the Columbia-Sciences Po dual degree are all there: four years at Exeter do not close the routes back to Europe, the United Kingdom or Canada.

Famous alumni

Exeter counts more than 23,000 alumni. A few of them, by graduating class:

  • Politics: Franklin Pierce (1820), fourteenth president of the United States; Daniel Webster (1796), Secretary of State; Robert Todd Lincoln (1860), Abraham Lincoln's son; Allen Dulles (1910), director of the CIA; and two candidates in the 2020 presidential primaries, Tom Steyer (1975) and Andrew Yang (1992).
  • Technology and business: Mark Zuckerberg (class of 2002), founder of Facebook, and Adam D'Angelo (2002), co-founder of Quora.
  • Literature: John Irving (1961), Gore Vidal (1943), Peter Benchley (1957), author of Jaws, and Dan Brown (1982), who later taught English at the school.
  • Science and economics: David Mumford (1952), Fields Medal; Lloyd Shapley (1940) and Paul Romer (1973), both Nobel laureates in economics.

Mathematics has a particular place in this history. Zuming Feng, a mathematics teacher at Exeter, coached the American team at the International Mathematical Olympiad from 1997 to 2013. And the library designed by Louis Kahn, opened in 1972, holds around 250,000 volumes and is often described as the largest secondary school library in the world.

What this means for your child

These results come from a school that selects strong students, then pushes them hard for four years. They say a lot about the level of the classroom and little about what a particular child will achieve. The useful question is not "how many Exeter students get into Harvard" but whether your child would grow in that environment, and how their college application would stand out among 312 classmates who are all very good. That is a question worth working on long before senior fall.
