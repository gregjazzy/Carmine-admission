Swiss boarding school fees: what each school costs, and which one for which child

Boarding schools October 2026 9 min read

A Swiss boarding school costs from just under CHF 70,000 to just over CHF 180,000 a year, depending on the school, before personal spending. That is a factor of nearly three, and it does not map neatly onto quality. Most of the gap comes from curriculum, language, size and what the fee actually covers.

I went through the fee schedules thirteen boarding schools publish on their own sites, and their terms and conditions where they exist, because that is where the lines missing from the headline figure are written.

Figures taken from the schools' websites and fee documents in October 2026, for seven-day boarding, from the youngest to the oldest year group. Le Rosey had already moved to publishing 2027-2028 only. Aiglon's site was down, hence the Good Schools Guide figure; Le Régent's in Crans-Montana was down too, and I would rather give no number than a guess.

The list falls into three bands. Around CHF 70,000, Swiss schools, mostly German-speaking, built first around the Swiss Matura, the national school-leaving diploma. Between CHF 100,000 and 135,000, mid-sized international boarding schools. Above CHF 160,000, the three names everyone knows, each covered in detail on this blog: Le Rosey, Aiglon and Beau Soleil.

Where the extra costs hide

The headline number is almost never what a family pays. Every school says so in its terms and conditions, just not in the same place or in the same way.

Then come the payments made on entry, which never appear in the annual fee. A deposit nearly everywhere: CHF 3,000 at TASIS, 12,000 at Brillantmont, 15,000 at Beau Soleil and Aiglon, 18,000 at Le Rosey, where it covers the extras account. Application fees from CHF 500 to 3,000. And capital contributions that families tend to forget: CHF 5,000 at TASIS, 10,000 at Leysin, a CHF 8,500 admission fee at St. George's, which also asks for a CHF 20,000 advance payment.

The last trap is in the contract. Several schools, Le Rosey, St. George's, Zuoz and Champittet among them, charge the following term if a student leaves mid-year; at Champittet, withdrawing after 31 October makes the whole year payable. At ENSR, paying in instalments adds CHF 2,000 a year.

Is there such a thing as a cheap Swiss boarding school?

Yes, depending on what "cheap" means. The realistic floor for private seven-day boarding in Switzerland is around CHF 70,000: the Ecole d'Humanité, Institut Montana, the Hochalpines Institut in Ftan, ENSR. That is a little under half of Le Rosey.

Three of those four are in German-speaking Switzerland, and the Matura there is taught in German, sometimes in a bilingual track. For a child who speaks French or English, the saving is paid for in language. The exception is ENSR in Lausanne, in French-speaking Switzerland, which prepares both the Matura and the IB for CHF 71,500 to 81,000 plus VAT; in my view that is the best value for a French-speaking student who wants to board in Switzerland.

Two options lower the bill for families living in or near Switzerland. Weekly boarding, home on Friday: CHF 57,500 at ENSR, 91,800 at Champittet. And the cantonal route: at Zuoz, the day Matura costs CHF 640 a year for families resident in Graubünden. Families living abroad have access to neither.

Below these amounts, what gets sold as a Swiss boarding school is usually a language course or a summer camp, not a school year.

Which school for which child

Once the budget is set, the child decides: their language, their temperament, the university they are aiming for. This is how I read the main options.
