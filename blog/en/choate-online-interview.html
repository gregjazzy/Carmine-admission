The Choate online interview

The Andrew Mellon Library, Choate Rosemary Hall, Wallingford. Photo Daderot, Wikimedia Commons, public domain

US boarding schools November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

Choate interviews every applicant online, once, and only online. Families ask the same questions about it every year. The answers follow, with what I have learned preparing children for it.

Is the interview required?

It is "strongly encouraged", and it must be completed in the current application cycle. In practice, a file without an interview gives the committee no direct contact with the child at all, in a process where the test is optional and there is no minimum grade. I do not know of a serious reason to skip it.

Why online only?

Choate does not hold interviews in person, on campus or anywhere else. The reason given is fairness: every applicant has the same experience, and every interview weighs the same, whoever the interviewer. The second reason is practical. A family visiting the campus is not divided between a tour and an appointment, and can give the visit its full attention.

For a family abroad this is good news: there is no journey to make for the interview. For the child it changes the exercise, which is the subject of the next questions.

How long does it last, and who is in the room?

The appointment is scheduled for forty-five minutes. For the first twenty to twenty-five, the interviewer speaks with the child one-on-one; the rest of the appointment is no longer a private conversation. Twenty minutes is long for a thirteen- or fourteen-year-old speaking to an adult they have never met, through a screen, without a parent nearby. It is also enough time for the interviewer to get past the prepared answers.

Twenty minutes that can be prepared

I work with the child online, in English, in real conditions, until they talk about what they think and what they love with ease and substance from the first minute.

When should it be booked?

Interviews run from September to February for entry the following fall. The invitation arrives by email after the inquiry form. Choate can accommodate only one interview per applicant, so the right moment is when the child is ready, not when the invitation arrives. A September interview with no practice is a wasted chance; a February interview squeezed between the January 15 deadline and the end of the season leaves no margin if a connection fails.

What is the interviewer looking for?

Choate's own words for what it wants to know are short: what the child thinks about, what they care about, what they do to relax, what they hope to explore next. None of these has a right answer. What separates candidates is whether there is a second sentence. "I like history" is an answer. "I have been reading about why the Ottoman Empire lasted so long, and I still do not understand how it held together" is the start of a conversation.

The question about relaxing is the one children underestimate. Many answer with something they think sounds serious. The interviewer is not grading the hobby; they are listening for a real person.

Can your child talk for twenty minutes, on screen, to an adult they do not know? Tell me about them.

What goes wrong on screen?

Short answers, first: good students answer the question asked, correctly, in one sentence, and leave the interviewer to carry the conversation. Then, eyes on the image rather than the camera, which reads as distance. And the silence after a question, which feels much longer online than in a room, so children fill it too quickly or freeze. All three improve with a few sessions of real practice, on the same kind of call, with someone the child does not know well.

How do you prepare without making them recite?

A child who has learned answers by heart is recognisable within minutes: the answers are too finished, and they do not survive a follow-up question. I work the other way round. With each child I find the two or three subjects they can talk about for ten minutes, then practise being interrupted, asked "why", and pushed off the script. The aim is a child who knows what they want to share and can follow the conversation wherever the interviewer takes it.

Who prepares the children I work with?

I do. I have students at Choate, and for seven years I have worked with around fifteen families a year aiming at the most selective schools in the United States, England and Switzerland. For Choate, the interview is usually where I start, because it is the piece of the file that most depends on the child, and the one that cannot be retaken.
