How to get into UNIS, the United Nations International School

United Nations International School, on the East River, Manhattan. Photo Transpoman, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Book a conversation

The United Nations International School was founded in 1947 by parents working at the UN, and it still looks like that idea: around 1,400 students from more than a hundred countries, in a concrete building on the East River at 25th Street, with half the families tied to the UN system and half simply New Yorkers who want an international education. It is one school from Pre-K to 12th grade, but it runs three quite different admissions processes depending on the age of the child, and a family that treats them as one usually loses time.

The three doors

UNIS is organised in three divisions, and each has its own way in.

  • Junior School, Pre-K to 4th grade. No test. The child spends time in a classroom with other children while staff watch how they engage, listen and play. Parents are not interviewed; they meet teachers at the same time.
  • Middle School, 5th to 8th grade. A standardized score (ISEE, SSAT or recent MAP results), plus group activities of about 45 minutes in 5th and 6th grade, or a 30-minute conversation in 7th and 8th.
  • Tutorial House, 9th to 12th grade. The same score options and a 30-minute individual conversation. Tutorial House leads to the IB Diploma, which UNIS helped to found in 1967.

Middle School starting in 5th grade rather than 6th matters more than it looks: it gives families a natural entry point a year earlier than most New York schools.

The route, step by step

  1. Open a Ravenna account. Everything happens there: the application, the documents, the booking of the campus meeting, the decision. Open it even if you are only considering UNIS; nothing else can start without it.
  2. Submit the application and pay the $75 fee. Families applying for financial aid can have it waived.
  3. Book the evaluation or meeting through the Ravenna calendar. For older children it is usually paired with a 45-minute tour.
  4. Request the documents. Current teachers complete the ISAAGNY form; schools send reports (from 2nd grade in Junior School, and for every Middle and Tutorial House applicant). Everything is due in January.
  5. Send a test score from 5th grade upward, ideally before January.
  6. Receive the decision in February.

What really decides

UNIS publishes no admission rate and no target score, and families who hunt for one are measuring the wrong thing. Entry is competitive in most grades without being ferocious, so the question is rarely "is my child good enough" and much more often "does the file say so clearly".

Three things carry the weight. The teacher form, because it is the only document written by someone who sees the child every day, and a lukewarm one is hard to overcome. The meeting on campus, where UNIS checks that the child will cope, in English, in a large and busy school that expects a good deal of independence. And, from 5th grade, the test score, which is the one piece that compares a child from Seoul with a child from the Upper West Side on the same scale.

Families linked to the UN, to a mission or a consulate, and families arriving from abroad can be given priority after the deadline or mid-year. Priority is not a guarantee of a place; it gets a file read when others are closed.

Common mistakes

Asking the current school for the teacher form in December, when teachers are swamped and the deadline is weeks away. Letting the school send MAP results by default without checking them. Choosing the entry grade by birthday alone, when a child coming from a system with a different school year may fit better one grade up or down: UNIS will place children from its French section on the recommendation of their previous school, and it is worth discussing the grade before applying rather than after. And preparing a teenager for the conversation with rehearsed answers, which an experienced interviewer spots within a minute.
