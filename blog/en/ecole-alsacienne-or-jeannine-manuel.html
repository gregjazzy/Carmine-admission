École alsacienne or Jeannine Manuel: how to choose

The École alsacienne, Paris 6th. Photo Celette, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Paris schools November 2026 6 min read

Many families apply to both. It makes sense: the École alsacienne and the École Jeannine Manuel are two of the most sought-after schools in Paris, secular, state-contracted, demanding, and both very hard to get into. But behind the resemblance lie two almost opposite philosophies, and a child does not thrive in the same way at one and the other.

I know both from the inside: I have followed Jeannine Manuel students for more than ten years, often among the top of their class, and I have students at the Alsacienne. Here is what really separates them.

Language: a project, or an opening

At Jeannine Manuel, bilingualism is the project itself: teaching in French and English throughout, a French international baccalaureate with an American option, and an IB track. From CE1 (age 7), the school turns away children who are beginners in English. At the Alsacienne, English is one language among others: German, Chinese, Italian, Spanish, Russian, Latin, Greek. English-speaking students have their own course, and the lycée offers European and Oriental sections. Students leave open to the world, but not bilingual by design.

Destinations reflect this. Half of Jeannine Manuel's class of 2024 went abroad, mostly to the United Kingdom and the United States. At the Alsacienne, it is nearly a quarter, and most students aim for the prépas, Sciences Po and the leading French universities.

Choosing, then getting in

I help you see which of the two truly suits your child, then prepare their application to one, the other or both, to the standard of schools that accept only a small share of their applicants. My aim: that they get into the one where they will grow best.

Selection: two ways of looking at a child

Both schools first sift by file. Then they diverge. Jeannine Manuel assesses the child in French, English and maths according to age, and meets the parents: from cinquième, the student attends the interview with them. The Alsacienne only tests in CM1 and sixième, in French and maths, and from CE1 no longer meets the parents: the child spends ten minutes alone with a teacher. One assesses a bilingual family project; the other, a child as they are.

Cost: a gap few families anticipate

Since 2026, the Alsacienne has set its fees by income, from free to about €4,340 a year for the highest incomes. At Jeannine Manuel, the state-contracted lycée costs more than €10,000 a year, and the IB track more than €30,000. For a well-off family, the difference over a full schooling is considerable; it should not decide on its own, but it deserves to be put on the table.

Torn between the two? Tell me about your child: I will tell you which one fits, and how to prepare each application.

For which child?

Jeannine Manuel suits a child who is already bilingual, or whose family makes bilingualism a project, academically at ease and seriously considering study abroad. The Alsacienne suits a curious child who needs a broad education and a climate without rankings to give their best, and whose horizon remains French first, without being limited to it. A brilliant child who is not at ease in English has little chance at Jeannine Manuel after CP; a child who needs the spur of rankings may be bored at the Alsacienne.
