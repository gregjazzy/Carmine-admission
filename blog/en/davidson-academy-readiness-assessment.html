The Academic Readiness Assessment

The University of Nevada, Reno campus, home of the Davidson Academy. Photo NevadaMarCom, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

US schools November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Qualifying test scores get a student invited; the Academic Readiness Assessment decides whether they are admitted. It is a day of work drawn directly from the Davidson Academy curriculum, designed to show whether a student is ready for the school's classes at their age and at that point of the year. It is scored by several raters, and when two raters differ by more than a couple of points, more are brought in.

I prepare students for this assessment. Here is how it is built.

The scoring

The Academy looks for 80 percent or better, and accepts students at 70 percent if the rest of the application, recommendations, grades, IQ results, is strong.

Writing and feedback

The Academy describes itself as a writing-across-the-curriculum school, and written expression is weighted accordingly. Its own figures are telling: fewer than 2 percent of applicants have had real instruction in expository or argumentative writing, and expectations are normed on that. A student who can make a debatable claim about a text, support it with two pieces of evidence, explain how the evidence supports it and conclude, stands out immediately.

Part of the reading and reasoning score is rated before and after instruction. Students receive feedback during the day, as in a real class, and those who use it to revise their work, rather than defend it, show exactly the readiness the school is looking for.

Math readiness

The math benchmark is specific. Seventh graders and younger must be ready for Algebra I, the Academy's lowest math class. Eighth graders must be ready for Geometry with good Algebra I skills. Ninth and tenth graders must be ready for Algebra II, with total mastery of Algebra I. Students are compared with current Academy students of the same age at the same point in the year, so an assessment in March expects more than one in September.

Work habits count

A quarter of the possible points go to affective traits. Proctors watch effort, attitude, the ability to follow directions and the willingness to ask for help. Students can work at their own pace through most of the day, but must stay focused throughout.
