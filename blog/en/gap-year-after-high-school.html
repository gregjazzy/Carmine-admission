Gap year after high school: how universities abroad, and Parcoursup, treat it

International Admissions October 2026 8 min read

Greg MitteletteFondateur de Carmine Admission. 1 100 élèves accompagnés en 14 ans, admis notamment à Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, l'EPFL et Bocconi.

Prendre rendez-vous

Fewer than 1% of students in France take a gap year, which the French call a césure. That figure comes from a report by France's education inspectorate published in May 2026, and the authors put it down to a school culture in which any break looks like failure. Harvard, meanwhile, has been reprinting a piece by its own admissions officers for over twenty years that argues the opposite. British universities sit somewhere in between: relaxed about a year out in most subjects, wary of it in a few.

For a student at a French lycée, at home or abroad, the useful question is less "will it hurt?" than "when do I apply?". You can apply in your final year and ask to defer the place, or apply during the year off with your baccalaureate results in hand. The two routes are treated quite differently from one country to the next.

France: Parcoursup lets you keep a place

Parcoursup is the national platform through which French students apply to almost all first-year university programmes. It has a formal gap-year procedure, and it works better than most families assume. When listing their choices, students tick a "césure" box. The programme only learns about it once the student has definitively accepted its offer, so it plays no part in ranking. The student then writes to the head of the institution explaining the project and how it will feed into the course they will join afterwards. A committee assesses it, and the request has to be confirmed again at enrolment.

If it is granted, the student signs an agreement that guarantees re-enrolment in the same programme afterwards, with no need to go back through Parcoursup unless they change their mind and want a different first-year course. They keep student status, pay reduced fees and hold a student card. The break lasts one semester or one academic year. France's ten-month national service follows the same route, through a dedicated "Service national" gap-year request.

It is not a right. The institution can refuse, with written reasons, and there is an internal appeal. The French education magazine L'Étudiant reports that most requests are accepted and that final answers usually arrive in mid-July. For a student weighing a selective French degree against an application abroad the following year, a Parcoursup gap year is an inexpensive way of holding a place in France. The destinations they will then choose between, with their cost, language and deadline, are compared in Study abroad after the French baccalaureate.

United States: Harvard encourages it, Stanford wants a plan

Harvard College's page on the subject is direct. The admissions committee encourages admitted students to defer for a year to travel, pursue a project, work or do something else meaningful. The page reprints Time Out or Burn Out, an essay first published in the New York Times in 2000 by three Harvard admissions officers and revised since, which makes the case against the exhaustion of American high schoolers. During the year, a deferred student may not enrol as a degree candidate anywhere else, and any formal study will not count towards a Harvard degree. Students defer through the admitted-student reply form, or, if they have already said they are enrolling, by the end of June at the latest.

Stanford is more structured. Admitted students request a deferral through their portal and must give "a clear plan" for the year, which the office reviews, replying within five to seven business days. Most deferrals last one year; two are allowed for national military service or a religious mission. Students also sign an agreement not to apply to, hold a place at, or enrol full-time at any other college while they hold the deferred place.

Princeton runs its own Bridge Year, launched in 2009: service placements abroad, in five countries according to the university's 2026 description, open to newly admitted students before their first year on campus. Yale has published a rare benchmark. In a normal year, 3 to 4% of admitted students take a gap year; in the summer of 2020 the figure was 20%.

Defer an offer, or apply during the year?

This is the real choice for the US. Deferring means being admitted in the final year of lycée on the normal timetable, which I set out in early decision, early action and regular decision. The student knows where they are going and spends the year without an application hanging over them.

Applying from a gap year is harder than it sounds. Parents on College Confidential who have done it describe the same problems: everything has to be redone, and the recommendation letters must come from a school the student has left, whose teachers have moved on to the next class. The window is short too, since applications go in between November and January, only a few months after the exams. Others point out that some merit awards and housing guarantees do not carry over to a deferred start, which has to be checked college by college. And Stanford's rule is typical rather than exceptional. A deferred place is not something to sit on while trying for a better one; the US forums are blunt with students who attempt it.

My advice to a French lycée student aiming at American universities is to apply in the final year as normal and decide on the gap year once admitted. A year off does not rescue a weak application. It suits a student who already has an offer and a reason to go.

United Kingdom: two routes, and one subject apart

UCAS offers deferred entry. When adding a course, the student picks a start date a year later and uses the personal statement to explain the plans for the year. Deferral is usually limited to one year, each university decides whether to allow it for that course, and the offer conditions do not move: the required baccalaureate grade must be achieved in the exam year. Holding a deferred place rules out applying elsewhere in the meantime.

The other route is a post-qualification application, made during the year out with results already known. Oxford students on The Student Room who went this way say it makes you "a known quantity": the university no longer has to bet on a predicted grade. For a French lycée, where staff are often uneasy about committing to predicted grades (a point I cover in UCAS with the French baccalaureate), that matters. The student will still need a reference from the old school, so it is worth arranging before leaving.

Mathematics is the exception. Cambridge's Faculty of Mathematics says most colleges prefer immediate entry because of the risk of going off the boil, and that some colleges are more welcoming of gap years than others. Students holding a deferred offer normally sit all their exams, STEP included, before the year out, and are told to keep up their mathematics during it. Girton goes further and expects evidence of continued mathematical work. At Oxford, the Department of Computer Science says deferred applications in Mathematics, Computer Science and their joint schools are considered from candidates with planned, structured activities. A volunteer adviser on The Student Room points out an odd consequence: the deferred applicant sits STEP in the exam year and then stops for twelve months, whereas the post-qualification applicant sits it at the end of the gap year and has no choice but to stay in practice.

Example: a student sitting the baccalaureate in June 2027 and aiming at Cambridge.

Deferred route: apply through UCAS before 15 October 2026, choosing 2028 entry; Cambridge lists its requirements "for entry in 2027 or deferred entry in 2028". For maths, sit STEP in June 2027 like direct-entry candidates.

Post-qualification route: apply in autumn 2027, during the gap year, for the same 2028 entry, with baccalaureate results known since July. UCAS publishes that cycle's dates in the spring.

And the French prépa?

The classes préparatoires, two-year intensive courses leading to the competitive entrance exams of the French grandes écoles, are not excluded by the rules, and L'Étudiant notes that gap-year requests are granted there too, though rarely. My own view is firmer. A good MPSI or ECG class expects students to arrive in September with the final-year syllabus fresh, mathematics above all, and a year without maths shows in the first weekly oral tests. If the plan is prépa, I would not take the year before it. The natural moment comes later, at the grande école, where a gap year before the final year is possible with the school's agreement.

How an admissions reader sees a year out

Put the official texts side by side and they ask for much the same thing. Stanford wants a plan, Parcoursup a letter linking the year to the course, Oxford structured activity, Cambridge mathematics kept alive. None of them gives marks for an exotic destination. What reads well is a year prepared before departure, with something verifiable at the end of it (an employer, an organisation, a certified language level) and some connection, even indirect, to the intended degree. Six months with a company in Singapore tells a reader something about a future economics student. Six months of travel with no thread tells them much less.

The mistakes students report are similar from one forum to the next. Leaving without a project and drifting, which one student interviewed by L'Étudiant called procrastinating. Letting maths slide before an admissions test. Holding a deferred place while applying elsewhere. And, on the French side, forgetting that the gap year must be confirmed at enrolment; a box ticked in March is not enough.

The inspectorate's report also found that nearly half of interested students give up for financial reasons. For the families we work with, money is rarely the issue. The order of decisions is: which country, apply in the final year or during the gap year, what written plan, and how to keep maths going if an admissions test lies at the end.
