Le Rosey: what is the best age to start?

The Château du Rosey, Rolle. Photo Eric Bajart, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

"When should we send them?" It is often the first question from families thinking about Le Rosey, and the most important. The age of entry does not only shape the child's experience; it also shapes their chances of admission. And on this, the school gives an answer few families expect.

Four stages, four doors

A Rosey education is divided into four sections. The Juniors, aged 8 to 12, live on the main campus in their own house, with the families of the teachers who live there, and follow a bilingual programme in which French dominates the early years. The Cadets, around 12 and 13, choose their main language and take at least one class in the other. The Jeunes Seniors spend two years preparing for the final exams. Finally, the Seniors take the International Baccalaureate or the French bac.

Many students arrive between 12 and 14 and stay until graduation. Some children are ready for boarding at 8, others are not; and the school reminds families that parents too must be ready, to let their child gain independence without trying to control everything from afar.

The right age, and the right preparation

I help you choose the moment when your child has the best chance of being admitted and being happy at Le Rosey, then prepare them for the exams and the campus day at the school's level. My aim: that they start at the right age, and ready.

The longer you wait, the narrower the door

The school writes it itself: from class 3, the equivalent of US grade 9, competition becomes increasingly intense, and admissions run in rounds. For the last two years it goes further: applying just to take the International Baccalaureate is possible, but it does not recommend it. Places are scarce, it turns away excellent applicants every year, and it doubts the value of spending only those two, busiest years at Le Rosey. It also notes that the younger siblings of students admitted at that level always apply earlier.

In my view, this is the most useful piece of information for a hesitant family: waiting "to be sure" often means applying when the chances are lowest.

What each age requires

Language requirements rise with age: among the Juniors, French or English is not essential; from 12, they must understand one; from 15, they must master one. At any age, the school expects the child to be able, in time, to follow the baccalaureate programmes. Starting young means fewer language requirements but a bigger adjustment to boarding; starting late means the opposite, with much stiffer competition on top.
