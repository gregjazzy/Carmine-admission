Harrow Sixth Form entry: up to 20 places at 16+

The Old Schools, Harrow School, Harrow on the Hill. Photo Bernard Burns, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Each September, Harrow admits up to 20 boys into Year 12, the Lower Sixth, alongside some 160 who have been there since the Shell. It is a small door, and the profile is explicit: boys likely to be predicted at least seven or eight GCSEs (or equivalent) at grades 7, 8 or 9, who will thrive as boarders, contribute inside and outside the classroom, show talent in their studies or in sport, the arts or another field, and have the potential to lead.

The file before any test

Registration asks for more than a form. A boy uploads his CV, with academic achievements and interests outside class; a one-page handwritten letter to the Head Master explaining why he wants to come to Harrow; his latest school report, with a short explanation of his school's grading system if it is not printed on the report; and the ID page of his passport. A confidential reference is requested from his current head.

The letter is the first thing that is truly his. It is short and handwritten, which rules out the polished essay a consultant might draft, and it is read by people who see hundreds. What stands out is specific: a subject he has pursued beyond the syllabus, a responsibility he has carried, a precise reason Harrow rather than another school. Generic praise of tradition and opportunity says nothing.

Four subjects, two rounds

A boy chooses the four subjects he intends to study at A level at Harrow, and he is tested in all four. Two tests come first, sat at his own school or at Harrow in the autumn of Year 11. If he performs strongly, he is shortlisted for the assessment at Harrow, where he sits the other two and is interviewed by senior staff and the Registrars, with a seminar led by a Head of Subject. Applicants at school overseas come for a Friday night and a Saturday in November; those in Hong Kong are tested and interviewed there.

The choice of subjects is therefore strategic. Where the boy has a say in which two come first, they should be his strongest, because they decide whether he gets to Harrow at all. And every subject must be one he genuinely intends to take, since the tests are pitched at a boy ready to start the A level course, and the seminar will expose a boy who chose a subject to look ambitious.

Coming from another system

A boy from the IB Middle Years, the French brevet or an American high school has no GCSE predictions to show, and Harrow asks for an explanation of his school's grading. Make that explanation do work: state where he stands in his class, what the top marks mean in his system, and which results compare with grades 8 and 9. The tests then follow A level expectations in each subject, which may differ in content and style from what he has met. Mathematics and the sciences in particular reward early practice on English-style questions.

What the interviews look for

Interviews with senior staff and the seminar with a Head of Subject test two things: intellectual engagement with the subjects he has chosen, and the kind of boy he will be in a House at sixteen, with younger boys looking up to him. Harrow wants Sixth Form entrants who will contribute and lead, not only those who will collect grades.

How I prepare Year 12 applicants

I work with the boy on his choice of four subjects and on the order in which to present them, prepare the subject tests in mathematics and the sciences at A level standard, and run mock interviews and seminars on his subjects until he can discuss them with the confidence of a future specialist. I also read his letter, not to rewrite it, but to make sure it says something only he could say.
