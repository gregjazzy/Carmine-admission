Aiglon College: IGCSE, IB and the Aiglon diploma

Villars-sur-Ollon, home of Aiglon. Photo Bledard92, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Aiglon teaches in English, following British and then international programmes: there is no French bac and no French curriculum. For a family coming from the French system, that is the first thing to understand, and often what decides the age of entry.

The IGCSEs, in Years 10 and 11

From 14 to 16, students prepare the IGCSEs, a British qualification recognised worldwide and marked outside the school. All study English, as a first language with literature or as a second language, maths, French and science, then choose three more subjects; the school suggests an art, a social science and a modern language. A student joining in Year 11 follows a pre-IB programme instead. In winter, everyone skis several times a week, and expeditions continue.

The International Baccalaureate, in Years 12 and 13

The final two years are those of the International Baccalaureate: six subjects graded from 1 to 7, and a core worth up to three points, for a maximum of 45. Students choose their subjects with the IB coordinator, already thinking about the universities and courses they are aiming for. Aiglon's results are among the best: in 2026, an average of 36.6 points, a 100% pass rate for the sixth year running, two students on 45 out of 45 and nineteen above 40. Classes have at most sixteen students, twelve on average.

Succeeding in the switch to an English-speaking system

I prepare your child to get into Aiglon and to succeed there: their written English, their working methods, the choice of IB subjects according to the universities they are aiming for. My aim: that the change of system becomes an advantage.

The Aiglon diploma

All students also work towards the school's own high school diploma. Some take only that: they attend IB classes and may sit some IB exams, but with fewer subjects and without Theory of Knowledge, the Extended Essay or CAS. That leaves time for what matters in their plans. It is a route worth knowing for a student for whom the full IB is not the best path.

What this means for a French student

The switch is real. Students must read, write and reason in English at a high level, and adopt methods different from those of the French collège. The later they arrive, the faster the gap must be closed. That is why, for a French-speaking child, the choice of entry year and preparation in English matter as much as admission itself.
