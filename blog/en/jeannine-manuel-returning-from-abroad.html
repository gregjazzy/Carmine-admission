Getting Into Jeannine Manuel When Returning From Abroad

Photo: École Jeannine Manuel

EJM Admission February 2026 · updated November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. Has worked with students of École Jeannine Manuel for more than ten years.

Book a conversation

Your child grew up abroad, speaks fluent English and has moved between cultures with ease. You are coming back to France, and École Jeannine Manuel seems the obvious choice: it is the school of international understanding and bilingualism. Admission should be straightforward.

That confidence is exactly what sinks applications. Every year, I see children with impeccable international backgrounds leave without a place. In more than ten years working with the school's students, and preparing families who return from abroad, the problem is almost never the child's potential. It is the gap between what the family thinks it is presenting and what the school expects.

What changes when you apply from abroad

A few of the school's rules work directly for or against a returning family:

  • No deadline for families living abroad who plan to settle in France, but the school recommends applying as early as possible: places go as decisions are made.
  • An adaptation programme takes children who do not yet speak French, from CE1 to troisième (ages 7 to 14). Places are allocated through the year, outside the usual calendar.
  • English, on the other hand, is required: no beginners from CE1, written English from CE2, a solid level in middle school, near-native English in lycée.
  • School reports that are neither in French nor in English must be translated by a sworn translator. A child at a private school outside the French national curriculum also provides a standardised test, if their school offers one.
  • The interview can take place in London, in New York or remotely.

An international background is not an advantage in itself

At Jeannine Manuel, a third of the students are non-French speakers and a third hold dual nationality. International experience is common there: it does not set an application apart. What matters is the child's ability to keep up in a school whose foundation remains French. It is under contract with the French state and follows the national curriculum, and that foundation is what unsettles children coming back from abroad.

The gap in mathematics

This is the point expatriate families underestimate most. The French curriculum teaches mathematics as reasoning: from middle school, students learn to write a proof and to separate hypothesis from conclusion. The American system, the IB's MYP or British GCSEs favour problem-solving and application. These approaches are equally valid intellectually, but none of them prepares a student for the written French-style proof.

A student who is brilliant at maths in the American system can be lost in front of a French-style geometry exercise, not because they do not understand the theorem, but because they do not know how to prove it according to the expected conventions. It is a problem of language, not of intelligence.

Has your child never written a French-style proof? I measure the gap with three tests at the school's level, in French, English and mathematics. Ask for an assessment.

Three starting points

A French school abroad

On paper, the most favourable situation: same curriculum, readable reports, French kept up. But French schools abroad do not all have the same level, and a 15 out of 20 in one is not necessarily worth a 15 out of 20 in a good Paris lycée. I see it when I assess these students: families often overestimate their child's level.

An international school, in the IB

The profile is appealing: international culture, used to working in English. But the MYP does not follow the same progression as the French curriculum in algebra and geometry, and its grades do not translate onto the French scale. The student has often never written a proof. Catching up is possible, provided you start well before the assessments.

A local system, American, British or other

The trickiest case. The child often has no landmarks in the French curriculum, and their written French, even if spoken at home, may have fallen well behind. If the child does not yet speak French, the adaptation programme is often the most realistic door. If they speak it but write it poorly, a transition year elsewhere is sometimes needed before applying.

What the school looks at beyond grades

The school says it looks for students able to benefit from its bilingual curriculum, and expects parents to have a strong international outlook and to be closely involved in their children's education. For a family returning from abroad, that is an asset, provided you show what those years brought: a child who adapted to several schools, came through difficult moments, and committed to activities. The file must tell this coherently, not just list countries.

Adaptability, resilience, commitment: have your child's file reviewed so that it tells a coherent story.

Catching up is not optional

The assessments cover French, English and mathematics. For a child coming back from abroad, English is almost never the problem: it is written French or method in mathematics. And English, however good, does not make up for a gap in the other two subjects.

This catch-up does not happen in two weeks of lessons before the test. It generally takes several months of targeted work, more for a child coming from a local system.

What I do for a returning family

I start by measuring the child's real level, not the assumed one: three tests at the school's level, in French, English and mathematics, and an interview, remotely if you are still abroad. We then decide on the year to aim for, whether the adaptation programme makes sense, and which schools to prepare in parallel. Then I prepare the child for the assessments and the interview, and follow the application until the answer.

Your child has lived what the school values. It still has to show, and their level has to keep up. For the full procedure, see the guide to getting into Jeannine Manuel.
