The Winchester College interview: what a Housemaster looks for

Winchester College, the Chapel and Chamber Court from above. Photo Tim Sheerman-Chase, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0

UK boarding schools November 2026 6 min read

Greg MitteletteFounder of Carmine Admission. 1,100 students supported over 14 years, with admissions to Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, EPFL and Bocconi.

Book a conversation

Shortlisted boys come to Winchester in January of Year 6 for an interview in person, by appointment, between 11 and 15 January for 2029 entry. The interviewer is a member of the pastoral care team, usually a Housemaster or a member of the Senior Management Team. The writing assessment takes place the same day. Of all the steps, the interview is the one where a boy is most himself, and the one families most often prepare in the wrong way.

Five qualities, named in advance

Winchester is unusually explicit: at interview, it looks for personal qualities as much as academic potential, and it names them. Curiosity, kindness, integrity, resilience, and a willingness to contribute to the life of a House. Each has a concrete form in a conversation with a ten-year-old.

  • Curiosity shows in follow-up: a boy who, after naming a book he liked, can say what surprised him in it, or what he went on to look up.
  • Kindness shows in how he talks about others: classmates, a younger brother, a teammate who made a mistake.
  • Integrity shows in honesty about himself, including saying "I don't know" without bluffing.
  • Resilience shows in a real story of something that went badly and what he did next.
  • Contribution shows in what he already does for a group: a choir, a team, a club he helps run.

Why a Housemaster cares

At Winchester, boarding is the centre of life. Each House holds 60 to 70 boarders, with 10 to 14 boys in each year group, a Housemaster, matrons, tutors who each look after 12 to 14 pupils, and its own kitchen and dining room where boys and dons eat together. Phones and devices are locked away for much of the day, so boys spend their free time together, offline. Someone who runs such a House is asking whether this boy will be good company at that table for five years, and whether he will grow there.

Preparing the Winchester interview

We prepare boys to talk naturally about what they read, what they care about and what they have learned from setbacks, so that the interviewer meets the real boy, at his best.

What the interviewer will have read

By January, the interviewer has the boy's school reports, the confidential reference and the CV he registered with. That CV is the most likely starting point. If it says chess, cricket and the cello, expect questions on each; if it lists ten activities, expect the interviewer to pick the least convincing one. Families should treat the CV as an honest map of what their son can talk about with enthusiasm.

Preparation that helps, and preparation that harms

Harmful: rehearsed answers to imagined questions, model replies about "why Winchester" learned from a website, a boy coached to mention Div because it sounds clever. An experienced Housemaster recognises a script within a minute, and it erases exactly the qualities he is looking for.

Helpful: regular conversations at home about what the boy reads and thinks, where he is asked "why?" and listened to; a few practice interviews with an adult he does not know, so that the format itself is no longer intimidating; and some thought about two or three real stories from his own life that show who he is. For boys educated in another language, practice in speaking at length in English about themselves makes a large difference.

After the interview

Offers, including any bursary award, are released in March of Year 6. Boys who accept are placed in a House during Year 7, where they meet their Housemaster, matron and future housemates, and are invited to taster lessons and activities.

How I prepare boys for the interview

I hold mock interviews in the style of a Housemaster's conversation, built on the boy's own CV and reading, and review them with him: where he was vivid, where he went vague, what example would have been stronger. We repeat until he can talk about himself with ease and ask a question of his own. Nothing is memorised; everything is his.
