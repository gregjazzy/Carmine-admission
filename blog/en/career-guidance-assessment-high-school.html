Career guidance assessment for high school students: four different services under one French name

Guidance October 2026 7 min read

Greg MitteletteFondateur de Carmine Admission. 1 100 élèves accompagnés en 14 ans, admis notamment à Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, l'EPFL et Bocconi.

Prendre rendez-vous

In France, what English speakers would call a career guidance assessment goes by the name bilan d'orientation. Parents looking for one, often because their teenager has to pick subjects or apply to university through Parcoursup, the national admissions platform, quickly find that the label covers very different things. A state psychologist at a guidance centre. A private coach running a personality questionnaire. A clinical psychologist assessing how a child thinks and concentrates. A teacher measuring academic level. All four are sold or offered as a bilan d'orientation, and they answer different questions at very different prices.

So I start from the parents' actual worry. "He has no idea what he likes" calls for one kind of assessment. "She wants to do a prépa, the two-year intensive course that leads to the top French engineering and business schools; will she cope?" calls for another.

The free option, and its limit

State guidance centres, the CIOs, are free and open to any pupil, including those at private schools. You are seen, usually by appointment, by a school psychologist trained in educational and career guidance. The same psychologists hold regular sessions in the state collèges and lycées of their area, so a state-school pupil can often meet one on site. A private-school pupil goes to the CIO.

What parents tend to report afterwards is that the meeting was interesting but too short to settle anything. That has more to do with workload than with the people: one psychologist covers several schools. Their remit is the school system: tracks, procedures, Parcoursup. Following which careers will still be in demand and well paid in fifteen years, which will disappear or change, and where value will be created, is not part of it. Yet that is the question parents ask me: what place their child will have tomorrow. If you go, bring the last two years of school reports and a question you can state in one line ("physics or economics as a specialty subject?"). "He doesn't know what to do" leaves the psychologist starting from zero. For the case where the child genuinely has no idea yet, I have written about where to begin; this article is about choosing the assessment itself.

Families abroad have no local CIO. Onisep, the state body for guidance information, runs a free service called Mon orientation en ligne, reachable by phone on +33 1 77 77 12 25, by chat and by email. It is good on procedures, less so for judging a student nobody there has met.

What an interest questionnaire cannot tell you

Read the testimonials that private firms publish and the same ending keeps coming back: a few sessions, a personality test, a career-interest test, then three suggested courses or careers, sometimes with a list of institutions, in a written report. Parents are often pleased, and I can see why. It gets the conversation going again at home.

The trouble is that those three paths come from what the student enjoys. They say nothing about whether the student can get in. And French guidance runs heavily on academic level. At the end of seconde (Year 11, or 10th grade), the class council makes recommendations on which three specialty subjects the pupil should take, and Onisep warns that grades in the subjects concerned weigh heavily. Since June 2026, every pupil in première (Year 12, or 11th grade) on the general and technological tracks also sits an early mathematics exam: two hours, no calculator, coefficient 2, and the ministry says the result goes into the Parcoursup file. A student who drops maths as a specialty is still judged partly on maths.

Every course listed on Parcoursup publishes what it expects of applicants, how it reviews applications and its admission rate. A suggested path that fails that test is a wasted choice. When a family shows me a guidance report, that is the first thing I check: can this student reach these courses with this record, or would it take two years of work? For prépas, the grade gaps between classes are set out in which prépa with which grades.

Timing it to the decision

An assessment delivered a week after the decision is wasted, and it is the scheduling mistake I see most often. The right moment depends on which choice is coming up.

Example: the Parcoursup calendar for the 2026 session.

Registration and entry of choices from 19 January to 12 March 2026, confirmation and complete files by 1 April 2026. Main admission phase until 11 July, supplementary phase opening on 11 June. Source: French Ministry of Education and parcoursup.gouv.fr.

For families moving back to France mid-lycée, the assessment often has to happen before enrolment in the new school, because the specialty subjects depend on it. The particular traps of that situation are covered in Parcoursup for returning expats.

Questions to ask before paying

First, who is doing it. In France the title of psychologist is protected by a law of 25 July 1985, and since 2024 psychologists are listed in the national RPPS register, searchable on the public Annuaire santé. "Guidance counsellor" in the private sector is not a regulated title; one firm says as much on its own website. Some independent counsellors set up an association, the COSI, in 2024 to give the field some structure. None of this tells you whether someone is good, but you will know who you are dealing with.

Second, which tests. A homemade forty-question online quiz is not a standardised test whose scores are compared with a sample of students of the same age. A serious practitioner will name the test and explain the choice.

Third, look at the final report. If it says nothing about the student's academic level, or about the gap between that level and the suggested courses, half the answer is missing. School reports alone will not fill that gap: a 14 out of 20 at a demanding Paris lycée and a 14 elsewhere tell different stories.

What should come out of it

A useful assessment ends with dated decisions: this specialty to confirm in May, this gap in maths to close before the summer, these open days to attend. Plenty of families stop there, and that is fine. Others want someone to carry the thread through to the applications themselves: the choice of courses, the timetable, the personal statements, the written tests and interviews where there are any. If your child is in seconde or première and you want to know when to start for their target, or would simply rather talk it through, write to me from the contact page.
