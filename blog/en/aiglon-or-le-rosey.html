Aiglon or Le Rosey: how to choose

Villars-sur-Ollon, home of Aiglon. Photo Bledard92, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Boarding schools November 2026 5 min read

Book a conversation

Aiglon and Le Rosey are the two names that come up most when a family talks about Swiss boarding schools, and many hesitate until the very end. From a distance they look alike: the same prestige, the same international reach, the same high standards. Up close, they shape children in quite different ways. Fifteen of my students have been admitted to Le Rosey, I have helped others into Aiglon, and every year I see families choose on image rather than on the child.

Language often decides before anything else

Le Rosey is a bilingual school: teaching happens in French and English, and students prepare either the International Baccalaureate or the French Baccalaureate, many learning two or three further languages on top. Aiglon is an English-speaking school leading to the International Baccalaureate. For a French-speaking child who wants to keep French as a working language, or who is aiming at the French bac, the question is almost settled. For a child who wants to move fully into an English-language education, Aiglon has the advantage of coherence.

Two ways of living the year

At Le Rosey every student boards, and the year is split between two campuses: the estate in Rolle, on Lake Geneva, and a village of chalets in Gstaad for the winter campus. Aiglon lives in the mountains all year round, above Villars, and takes some day students: about three in four students board. Classes average twelve, and the school has around four hundred senior and a hundred junior students, from more than sixty-five nationalities.

The tone differs too. Le Rosey shapes its students through a highly structured community life, clear codes of conduct and constant cultural openness. Aiglon, founded in 1949 by John Corlette on the balance of mind, body and spirit, also shapes them through physical effort, the mountains and the independence they demand.

Two admissions that call for different preparation

During a day on campus, Le Rosey tests mathematics, the working language and the mother tongue, decides weekly for younger children and in rounds for the upper classes, and applies strict nationality quotas, which makes timing decisive. Aiglon collects two confidential teacher recommendations, holds an interview and assessments focused on reasoning, logic and literacy, and makes decisions every month. A family applying to both has to keep two rhythms and two separate preparations going.

Visits differ as well. Aiglon offers an initial visit of about an hour and a half before any application, then a full admissions visit of three to four hours once the file is in. At Le Rosey, the child discovers the school on the very day of their papers, during the campus day. For a hesitant family, that means you can see Aiglon before choosing, and you discover Le Rosey by applying.

Age matters more than families think

Both schools take young children: Le Rosey's Junior Section is built for eight- to twelve-year-olds, and Aiglon has around a hundred junior students. Many Roséens arrive between twelve and fourteen and stay until they graduate, and competition for places at Le Rosey grows noticeably tougher from class 3, the equivalent of US Grade 9. A family that waits too long can find the door narrower than expected at either school.

The right child for each school

In my view, Le Rosey particularly suits a child who wants to stay bilingual, thrives within clear rules and enjoys a very international group life. Aiglon suits a child who wants to immerse themselves in an English-language curriculum, loves physical effort and the outdoors, and grows when given responsibility. Both achieve excellent results: Aiglon's IB average was 36.6 points in 2025. Prestige does not separate them. The child does.
