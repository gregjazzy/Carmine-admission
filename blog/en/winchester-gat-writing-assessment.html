Winchester College: General Ability Testing and the writing assessment

Two assessments sit at the heart of Winchester's 13+ process, and they could hardly be more different. The first is an online General Ability Test, sat in November of Year 6. The second is a piece of handwritten writing produced in Winchester in January, on the same day as the interview, by boys who have passed the first round. One is adaptive and multiple-choice; the other is open and slow. Together they tell the College how a boy thinks.

The General Ability Test

For entry in 2029, the GAT takes place between 16 and 20 November 2026. It is taken online and administered by Winchester staff. Boys are then invited to interview on the strength of two things: their application (reports, references, CV) and their GAT result.

The guide Winchester gives parents describes a computer-based baseline assessment: adaptive, so that each question adjusts to the level of the previous answers; around fifty minutes long; and built not around school subjects but around cognitive ability, through vocabulary, mathematics and non-verbal skills. Each section opens with untimed examples that show the format, and the examples can be repeated. Practice versions of the test itself cannot be bought by families.

What that means for preparation

A baseline test of this kind is designed to resist cramming, and Winchester insists that its assessment should not require tutoring. Take that seriously, and look at what the three areas reward. Vocabulary is built by years of reading, not weeks of word lists. Mathematics in an adaptive test rewards fluency and number sense: a boy who calculates confidently in his head moves up to the harder questions. Non-verbal reasoning rewards a calm eye for patterns, and a child who has never seen such questions loses time on the first few.

Sensible preparation is therefore light and early: a habit of serious reading from Year 5, mental arithmetic kept sharp, and enough exposure to non-verbal puzzles that the format is familiar. Anything heavier risks a tired boy on the day.

The Winchester Writing Assessment

Boys invited to interview also complete the writing assessment on the same day in Winchester. It is an extended response to stimulus material, written by hand on paper, and it is designed to assess creativity and critical thinking. Whatever the stimulus, what matters is what the boy does with it.

That format is unusual for a ten- or eleven-year-old. Most children write at school in short, guided exercises, or type. Here a boy faces a blank page, a prompt that has no single right answer, and the clock. The ones who do well tend to share three habits: they take a minute to decide what they think before writing; they make a point and develop it with something concrete, an example or a detail from the stimulus; and they write legibly enough to be read with pleasure.

Your son writes well on a keyboard but rarely by hand? Let's talk about it; it is quick to fix.

Why the two together

The GAT gives Winchester a measure that compares every applicant on the same scale, whatever his school, his country or his curriculum. The writing assessment shows what no multiple-choice test can: whether a boy has ideas of his own and can hold them on a page. A high GAT with a thin piece of writing, or a lively essay with a weak GAT, tells the College different things, and the interview that follows the same day completes the picture.

How I prepare boys for the GAT and the writing assessment

I work in two directions. For the GAT, short sessions on non-verbal formats and mental mathematics, and above all a reading plan that broadens vocabulary without feeling like homework. For the writing assessment, timed handwritten responses to varied stimuli, a text one week, a picture the next, with detailed feedback on structure, argument and style. The aim is a boy who arrives in Winchester with ideas, and the habit of writing them down well.
