Selective schools in Canada

The UTS interview stations

Applicants who do well on the UTS Entrance Test are invited, on January 8, 2027, to Stage 2. On January 23 they come to the school for interviews and a group task. The interviews are not a conversation with an admissions officer about grades and hobbies. They are a series of short, structured stations, modelled on the multiple mini-interviews that McMaster University designed for its medical school.

I prepare candidates for this format, which surprises most families the first time they hear about it. Here is how it works.

The format

Each applicant rotates through five to six stations in about an hour. At each one, an interviewer asks a question or presents a scenario, and the child has a few minutes to respond. Then they move on, to a different interviewer and a different situation. No single station decides the outcome; the picture comes from all of them together.

Before the day, UTS holds a virtual information session on January 16 for invited families. It also publishes sample interview questions drawn from past years, for all grades, without model answers.

What the stations assess

The test in December measures academic ability. The stations measure everything else. UTS names six qualities: cultural sensitivity, maturity, teamwork, empathy, reliability and communication. The questions are designed to let a child express themselves authentically and clearly, and to show what makes them a distinct candidate.

In practice, a station might ask a child to explain how they would handle a disagreement between friends, to respond to a situation where someone has been treated unfairly, or to give an opinion on a question that has no right answer. The interviewer is listening for how the child thinks: whether they consider other people's points of view, whether they reason before concluding, whether they can say what they would actually do rather than what sounds good.

Why bright children stumble

Strong students are used to questions with correct answers. A station that asks what they would do if a teammate kept missing practice has no correct answer, and a child who searches for one tends to give a short, cautious reply. Others go the other way and deliver a polished, general speech that never touches the actual situation. Both miss what the format rewards: a concrete, considered response that weighs the people involved and lands on a decision.

Time matters too. A few minutes per station is enough to think aloud, consider two sides and conclude, but not enough to wander. Children who have never practised under that constraint often run out of time before reaching their point.

Preparing without scripting

Memorized answers do not survive this format: the scenarios change and the interviewers follow up. Useful preparation gives a child a way of approaching any scenario, understanding the situation, considering who is affected, weighing options and saying what they would do and why, and then practises it on many different situations under real timing. Discussing news and everyday dilemmas at home, as UTS itself suggests, builds the same habit.
