Jeanine Manuel Waitlist: What to Do

EJM Admission February 2026 8 min read

You open the email from Ecole Jeanine Manuel. It is neither the acceptance you hoped for nor the rejection you feared. Your child has been placed on the waitlist. And now you are in a particular kind of limbo -- one that is psychologically excruciating and strategically delicate. Here is what you need to know, what you should do, and what you absolutely must avoid.

First: what the waitlist actually means

Being waitlisted at Jeanine Manuel is not a polite rejection. It is a genuine holding position. The school has evaluated your child, found them admissible, but does not currently have a spot available. This distinction matters enormously, because families who interpret the waitlist as a soft "no" often make decisions they later regret -- withdrawing prematurely, stopping engagement, or failing to maintain their candidacy.

EJM does not waitlist candidates as a courtesy. The school's admissions process is direct: candidates who do not meet the bar are rejected outright. If your child is on the waitlist, it means the school genuinely considered admitting them and may still do so. That is meaningful information.

Why spots open up

Understanding the mechanics of the waitlist requires understanding why admitted families decline their offers. There are several common reasons:

  • Competing offers: families who applied to multiple schools -- EJM, Saint-Germain-en-Laye, Franklin, international schools abroad -- sometimes choose another option. This is the most common source of waitlist movement.
  • Relocation changes: expatriate families whose professional situations shift between the admission decision and the start of the school year. A family that accepted in March may announce in May that their relocation to Paris has been postponed or cancelled.
  • Financial reconsideration: EJM is a private school with significant tuition costs. Some families who accept in the enthusiasm of admission later conclude that the financial commitment is unsustainable.
  • Personal circumstances: family situations change. Divorces, job losses, health issues -- life intervenes, and spots open.

The result is that waitlist movement is real but unpredictable. In some years, significant movement occurs. In others, very little. There is no way to predict which year you are in.

What you should do: the LOCI strategy

LOCI stands for Letter of Continued Interest, and it is the single most important tool available to a waitlisted family. The LOCI is a brief, carefully crafted communication to the admissions office that accomplishes three things:

1. Reaffirm genuine interest

The school needs to know that if a spot opens, your family will accept it. Waitlist management is logistically complex. The admissions team does not want to offer a spot to a family that has moved on, because that wastes one of the precious few spots that become available. Your LOCI must make it unambiguous that EJM remains your first choice and that you will accept an offer immediately.

2. Provide meaningful updates

If anything has changed since the application was submitted -- new academic achievements, a relevant extracurricular development, a language certification, a significant personal experience -- the LOCI is the place to communicate it. But the updates must be genuine and material. Do not fabricate developments. Do not inflate minor activities. One substantive update is worth more than a list of trivial additions.

3. Demonstrate maturity and grace

The tone of the LOCI matters as much as its content. It must be respectful, concise, and free of desperation. The school is evaluating your family's temperament as much as your child's qualifications. A LOCI that is demanding, emotional, or excessively long sends the wrong signal. One page. Professional tone. Clear message.

The LOCI should be sent approximately two to three weeks after the waitlist notification. A single follow-up, if the wait extends significantly, may be appropriate in late May or early June. More than that crosses the line from engaged to intrusive.

What you must NOT do

The waitlist period brings out the worst impulses in anxious parents. I have seen families sabotage their candidacy through well-intentioned but catastrophically misguided actions. Here is what to avoid at all costs.

Do not call the admissions office repeatedly

One polite inquiry to confirm receipt of your LOCI is acceptable. Weekly calls to "check on the status" are not. The admissions team manages hundreds of files simultaneously. Frequent contact does not demonstrate interest. It demonstrates a lack of boundaries. And it creates a negative association with your family's name -- the opposite of what you want when the team is deciding which waitlisted candidate to offer a spot to.

Do not attempt to lobby through connections

This is France, and the instinct to activate networks is strong. But having a board member, a current parent, or a well-connected friend call the school on your behalf is more likely to hurt than help. EJM prides itself on the integrity of its admissions process. External pressure is perceived as an attempt to circumvent that process. It generates resentment, not favor.

There is a narrow exception: if a current EJM family who genuinely knows your child offers to write a spontaneous, unsolicited letter of support, that can be marginally helpful. But this must be organic, not orchestrated. The school can tell the difference.

Do not panic

Panic leads to bad decisions. Families who panic withdraw from the waitlist prematurely, commit irrevocably to a backup school before the waitlist has resolved, or transmit their anxiety to their child in ways that are psychologically damaging. The waitlist is a process. It has a timeline. It will resolve. Your job is to manage that timeline strategically, not emotionally.

The timeline: what to expect

The waitlist at EJM follows a broadly predictable rhythm, though exact dates vary by year:

  • Late March / Early April: initial admission decisions are communicated. Waitlist notifications go out simultaneously or shortly after.
  • April: admitted families have a deadline to confirm their acceptance. This is when the first round of waitlist movement typically occurs as some families decline their offers.
  • May: a second wave of movement sometimes occurs as family situations crystallize -- relocations are confirmed or cancelled, financial decisions are finalized, competing school deadlines force choices.
  • June: the waitlist effectively closes. By mid-June, the school has finalized its class composition for the following September. If you have not received an offer by this point, the answer is no for this cycle.

The wait is long. Two to three months of uncertainty is standard. Families must prepare psychologically for this duration and resist the urge to force a resolution before the process has run its course.

Plan B must be activated immediately

This is the most important strategic principle of waitlist management: hope for the best, plan for the alternative. The moment you receive a waitlist notification, you must -- without hesitation -- activate applications to backup schools. Waiting to see how the waitlist resolves before engaging with alternatives is a potentially catastrophic error.

The schools to consider as alternatives depend on your family's profile and priorities:

  • Lycee International de Saint-Germain-en-Laye: the most natural alternative for families seeking an international section within the French system. Admissions run on their own timeline, and applications should be submitted as early as possible. For a detailed comparison, see our article on Jeanine Manuel vs Saint-Germain-en-Laye.
  • Lycee International Francois 1er (Fontainebleau): another public option with international sections, less competitive than Saint-Germain but offering a solid bilingual pathway.
  • EABJM (Ecole Active Bilingue Jeanine Manuel): not to be confused with EJM itself, this is a separate bilingual institution with its own admission process and culture.
  • Franklin (Lycee International de Paris Honore de Balzac): a public lycee in Paris with an international section, offering the BFI.
  • IB schools: for families open to the International Baccalaureate pathway, schools like the International School of Paris (ISP) or the British School of Paris offer alternative routes to international higher education.

Securing a place at a backup school does not close the door to EJM. If the waitlist produces an offer, you can still accept it. But having a confirmed alternative eliminates the existential anxiety that makes the wait unbearable and prevents your family from finding itself without a school in September.

Preparing for reapplication

If the waitlist does not produce an offer, the question becomes: should you reapply? The answer, in most cases, is yes -- but with a fundamentally different approach.

Reapplication works when the candidate addresses the gaps that kept them from a direct admission. This requires an honest assessment of what those gaps were. Was the academic French insufficient? Were the math test scores below the threshold? Was the interview unconvincing? The school will not tell you explicitly, but an experienced consultant can often reverse-engineer the likely reason from the candidate's profile and the feedback available.

A reapplication that is substantively identical to the first application will produce the same result. The school remembers. What they want to see is growth -- in language proficiency, in academic performance, in maturity, or in the coherence of the application narrative. One year of targeted development, with specific preparation for the areas of weakness, can transform a waitlisted candidate into an admitted one.

Managing the emotional dimension

I will close with something that admissions guides rarely address but that matters enormously: the waitlist is hard on your child. Teenagers understand what is happening. They know they were not accepted outright. They may feel that they were "not good enough." They watch friends receive admissions to other schools while they remain in limbo.

How you, as a parent, frame this experience will shape how your child processes it. The waitlist is not a judgment on their worth. It is a logistical reality of a school with more qualified applicants than available spots. Your child was found admissible. The constraint is space, not merit. Communicate this clearly, calmly, and repeatedly.

And whatever happens -- whether the waitlist converts to an offer or not -- your child's educational trajectory is not determined by a single school's decision. The school is a lever, not a destination. The student who develops discipline, curiosity, and resilience will thrive wherever they land.
