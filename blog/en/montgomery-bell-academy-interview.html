The Montgomery Bell interview

At Montgomery Bell, a personal interview is required of every applicant. It is part of the fifth and last step of the process, alongside the supplemental materials, and the school holds a Prospective Student Interview Day on February 6. For many boys applying to the Junior School, it is the first real interview of their life.

Why MBA insists on it

MBA's admission philosophy is that a boy's work and character should define him. A test score and a transcript show the work. Character, the school can only judge by meeting the boy. The interview and the shadow visit are the two steps where it does that directly, and the interview is the one where the boy speaks for himself, one to one, with an adult.

The nine qualities in the background

MBA describes its graduate in a Portrait of a Graduate built on three ideals, each with three qualities:

GentlemanScholarAthlete
IntegrityCuriosityResilience
CourageCreativityConsistency
KindnessUnderstandingTeamwork

No interviewer will ask a boy to rate himself on these. But the best answers naturally touch them: a time he kept going when something was hard, a question he could not stop thinking about, a teammate he helped.

Ready to talk about himself

I prepare boys for the MBA interview in short, real conversations, until they can talk about what they do and what they care about with ease and substance, not from a script.

What a good answer sounds like

"I play soccer" is a fact. "I was moved from forward to defense this year, and I hated it until I realised I could read the game better from the back" is a conversation. The difference is not intelligence or polish; it is the habit of saying the second sentence. Most boys of twelve or thirteen stop after the first. A few sessions of practice change that more than any list of model answers.

Does your son answer in one sentence, or in three? Tell me about him.

What to avoid

Learned answers, which an experienced interviewer spots in a minute. Parents answering for the boy, or briefing him so heavily that he sounds like them. And the opposite mistake, sending a boy in with no idea what to expect: a short conversation beforehand about the kind of questions he may hear removes most of the nerves.

How I prepare boys for it

I have students at Montgomery Bell, and for seven years I have prepared around fifteen families a year for the most selective schools in the United States, England and Switzerland. With an MBA candidate, I find two or three subjects he can talk about at length, then practise with follow-up questions he has not heard before, so that by February he is used to being asked "why?" and enjoys answering.
