CPES at Henri-IV: admission rate and what the committee looks at

The CPES (cycle pluridisciplinaire d'études supérieures) was created in 2012 by PSL University and the Lycée Henri-IV, a Paris state lycée on the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève. It is a three-year bachelor's programme that borrows its intensity and small classes from the French prépas (the two-year intensive programmes for the top grandes écoles), and its freedom to specialise and research training from the university. For a very strong student torn between a prépa and a university degree, it is often the option that ticks the most boxes. It is also highly selective, and it reads an application in a way that favours those who know how.

Every year I support students applying to the most selective higher education programmes, and I know from the inside the lycées the applicants come from: the way each one grades weighs directly on this application.

CPES Henri-IV: the admission rate

PSL states in its FAQ for September 2026 entry that its multidisciplinary bachelor's programmes have a selectivity rate of 4%, and that about 240 students are admitted to the first year, across five tracks. Three of them spend their first year at the Lycée Henri-IV: Sciences; Humanities and Society; Economics, Society and Law. A fourth, Data Science, Arts and Culture, is taught at the Lycée Louis-le-Grand. The fifth, Sciences for a Sustainable World, is taught mainly in English, on a PSL campus in the 5th arrondissement.

Applications are made per track, on Parcoursup, France's national higher-education application platform, and each track counts as one choice. The choice is final: students cannot switch tracks after enrolment. The CPES sits in Parcoursup's selective bachelor's category, separate from the prépas.

What the admissions committee looks at

PSL describes its method with unusual precision. The committee relies first on the fiche Avenir, the form completed by the student's school: final-year grades subject by subject, the class average, and teachers' comments. It looks first at grades and class rankings in the subjects of the chosen track. School reports come as a complement. The cover letter is compulsory and read "carefully", to check that the student knows the programme and has a genuine interest in its subjects.

There is no minimum average, and PSL explains why: averages vary too much from one school to another. What counts is strength across all the track's subjects, since the programme is multidisciplinary, and the ranking within the class. PSL sums up the typical profile in one sentence: students who are usually top of their class and solid in every subject of the chosen track.

In practice, a student brilliant in maths but weak in humanities has little chance in Humanities and Society, and the reverse holds for Sciences. And rank counts as much as the grade: a 16 out of 20 in a class averaging 15 does not mean the same as a 16 in a class averaging 11.

Grades and rank in the track's subjects, a letter read with care: let's review your child's file and prepare the cover letter.

Maths specialism required in four tracks out of five

This is the point many families miss: the maths specialism of the French baccalauréat is required in Sciences, in Economics, Society and Law, in Data Science, Arts and Culture, and in Sciences for a Sustainable World. The second specialism depends on the track: a science specialism for Sciences, ideally economics and social sciences or geopolitics for Economics, Society and Law. In Humanities and Society the specialisms are open, and the Parcoursup listing cites philosophy and literature (HLP) and geopolitics (HGGSP) as examples. In every track PSL expects a good level in maths; the common expectations call for a very good level in maths, the ability to reason rigorously, and a recommended B2 level in English, with C1 in French.

A humanities-minded student who drops the maths specialism at the end of the penultimate year therefore rules out four of the five tracks, even though they may have had the profile. That decision is made in the spring of the penultimate year, when students choose their two final-year specialisms, and that is when it needs thinking through.

Keeping the maths specialism or not is decided in the spring of première. Let's talk about your child's plans before that choice, which opens or closes four tracks.

CPES or prépa: what separates them

PSL is explicit: the CPES does not prepare for the grandes écoles' competitive exams and does not replace a prépa. It is a complete three-year programme leading to a university diploma with bachelor's degree status. Afterwards, the FAQ reports 100% of graduates continuing to a master's or a grande école. Those aiming for the ENS or an engineering school enter through the routes reserved for university students, on file, sometimes with an interview or tests, not through the prépa competitive exams. For a business school, PSL rather recommends a dedicated prépa.

The pace is that of a prépa: twenty-five to thirty hours of classes a week in first year, classes that rarely exceed forty students, work to prepare between sessions. The cost is that of a French public university: about €178 in annual tuition plus a €105 student life contribution, and free for scholarship holders.

What the application says, and what it does not

PSL sets no minimum average, because averages vary too much from one lycée to another. So it is the ranking, the class average and the school's reputation that give the grades their meaning. A 15 earned in a lycée that grades harshly can weigh more than a 17 earned elsewhere, and the reverse is true too. Teachers' comments count as much as the figures, and not every lycée knows how to write them for a committee of this level.

I have followed students in the leading lycées for years: I know how each one grades, what its rankings are worth, and how its applications are received. That is what makes it possible to build an application that reads as it should, with no weak spot in a subject of the track, and a cover letter showing that the student knows the programme, its majors, its research training, and why this track rather than a prépa.
