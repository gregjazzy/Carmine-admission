Families preparing for the SHSAT soon learn a number: the Stuyvesant cutoff. For 2026 admission to 9th grade, it was 561. It is worth understanding exactly what that number is, because it is often misread as a pass mark.

A cutoff is a result, not a rule

Seats are given in descending score order. The highest scorer gets their first choice, then the next, and so on until a school fills. The cutoff is simply the score of the last student who received a seat at that school. It is known only after the fact, and it depends on how many students test, how they score, how they rank the schools, and how many seats there are. That is why it changes from year to year.

Why Stuyvesant's cutoff is the highest

Stuyvesant fills first because many of the highest scorers list it as their first choice. By the time its seats are gone, students with slightly lower scores are already being placed at their second or third choices. A student who would have cleared Bronx Science's cutoff but ranked Stuyvesant first still gets Bronx Science if they listed it second; ranking does not cost a seat elsewhere.

What the score is made of

The composite score adds two scaled scores, one for English Language Arts and one for Mathematics. From fall 2026, the test is computer-adaptive, but the conversion from performance to scaled scores follows the same principle as before. A weak section cannot be hidden: both count equally in the sum.

Using the cutoff sensibly

  • Treat past cutoffs as indications of the level, not targets.
  • Aim for a margin above the cutoff of your first choice, because the line moves.
  • List every testing school your child would attend, in true order of preference: an unlisted school can never make an offer.
