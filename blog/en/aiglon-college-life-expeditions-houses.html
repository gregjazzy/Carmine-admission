Aiglon College: expeditions, houses and boarding life

Villars-sur-Ollon, home of Aiglon. Photo Bledard92, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Boarding schools November 2026 5 min read

Book a conversation

To tell whether a child is right for Aiglon, I look less at their grades than at the week they will live. Above Villars, the mountains are not a backdrop: they are part of the curriculum, and at admission the school checks that a candidate is ready to live them. I have helped students win places at Aiglon and Rosenberg, and fifteen at Le Rosey, and it is often daily life, more than academic level, that decides whether a child will thrive there.

A day at Aiglon

The day starts early, around seven, with the house morning routine. Before the first lesson, two or three mornings a week, the school gathers for meditation; on other days there is assembly or tutor time. Lessons run until mid-afternoon, followed by two activity sessions from 3.30 to 5.30 pm. Dinner is taken by house, followed by a house meeting, then supervised evening prep, with student and tutor meetings. Older students have some free time before returning by 9.30 or 10 pm depending on age; in the winter term, everything moves thirty minutes earlier.

The pace is intense, but it is designed as a balance between hard work, personal challenge, relaxation and community life. One detail says a lot: on Mondays, the whole school eats vegetarian, a long-standing tradition.

The house, a second family

Aiglon has ten houses, five for girls and five for boys: eight in the senior school, most with around fifty students, and two in the junior school, with up to thirty. Day students are fully part of house life. Each house has its houseparent, its team, its traditions and its character, and inter-house competitions, from football to debating, punctuate the year. For a child arriving from far away, this is where everything happens: friendships, mutual support, and learning shared responsibility.

Expeditions, the soul of the school

Every student goes on expeditions regularly, from the junior school to graduation: mountaineering, mountain biking, cross-country skiing, ski touring, rock climbing. The programme is progressive and builds year on year. At the top are the Skyline expeditions, the most challenging ascents, which lead to the school's own Skyline Award; many students also take the Duke of Edinburgh's International Award. The aim is not to train mountaineers, but to help children grow through real experience, and each year individual students receive awards for outstanding commitment and progress. For a child who has never slept in a mountain hut, the first expedition is a real step; by the final years, it has become part of who they are.

Meditation, the founder's legacy

Since the school's founding, morning meditation has taken the place of the assembly found elsewhere. Each session begins and ends in silence, around a single idea presented by a teacher or a student: a life choice, social media, a spiritual question without being religious. For some children it is the best moment of the day; for others, a puzzling discovery. Better to know beforehand.

Which child is it for?

Aiglon suits a child who wants to push themselves physically, enjoys group life and accepts a demanding framework, and wants to grow through experience as much as through study. It suits less a child who looks first for comfort, or whom cold, altitude and nights outdoors worry more than they attract. That is what the interview is looking for, and what a child must be able to express sincerely.
