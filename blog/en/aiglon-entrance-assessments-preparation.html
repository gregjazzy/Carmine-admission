Aiglon College: should you prepare for the entrance assessments?

Villars-sur-Ollon, home of Aiglon. Photo Bledard92, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Boarding schools November 2026 5 min read

Book a conversation

Aiglon describes its entrance assessments as an "honest reflection" of the child: nothing to revise, just be yourself. Many families take that literally and arrive on the mountain unprepared. It is the most common mistake I come across. I have helped students win places at Aiglon and Rosenberg, and fifteen at Le Rosey, and every year I prepare children for the entrance tests of the leading Swiss boarding schools: these assessments can be prepared for, just not like a school exam.

Three formats depending on age

For the youngest applicants, in Years 3 and 4, the assessment takes the form of a "Wonder Years experience": the child joins a lesson with other pupils, plays at break time, then writes an independent piece, reads, and completes a short maths quiz. If they cannot come to Villars, they have a short online conversation with their parents present and several online quizzes.

From Year 5 to Year 11, the application includes an interview and several online assessments focused on problem solving, mathematical reasoning, logical thinking and literacy. In Year 12, coming to campus is compulsory: an introduction to the school, a tour, an interview, online assessments, and a mathematics test on general topics, with a scientific calculator provided.

An "honest reflection", and what lies behind it

Aiglon wants to understand a child's strengths and spot where they will need extra guidance. That is true, and reassuring. But an honest reflection is not a lenient one: the school is measuring whether the child can follow a demanding curriculum, in English, in a mountain boarding school that asks for a great deal of independence. A child who meets the format for the first time on the day, who has never tackled a logic problem outside their textbook, or who rarely writes in English, gives a faithful picture, but of a level below their own.

The assessments say little about what is expected at each age, or about how the results weigh against the interview. That is where the difference is made, and it is what I know, from preparing candidates and following students at the school.

What trips up a strong candidate

Language comes first. Teaching is in English, and the literacy component measures reading and writing, not conversation. A child who is bilingual in speech but schooled in French often loses marks on an independent written piece, the very task families assume is easiest.

Reasoning comes next. Problem solving and logic are not taught in any school syllabus. A very good student used to textbook exercises can be at a loss in front of a question with no familiar method.

Excellent grades, but never seen this kind of question? Tell me about your child.

Finally, the Year 12 mathematics test. A student from the French system masters proof but has rarely used a scientific calculator on questions in English; an IGCSE student handles it well but justifies less. The test covers general topics: speed and ease make the difference.

Preparing without cramming

The point is not to memorise answers. It is for the child to know the format, to have met logic and reasoning problems, to write confidently in English, and to be able to talk about themselves at the interview, where questions cover character, academics, interests, friendships and their view of boarding life. A child prepared this way is not playing a part: they arrive with their real abilities.

Getting the timing right

The full admissions visit, with interview and assessments, lasts three to four hours and can only be booked once the application is in and the CHF 3,000 registration fee is paid. Decisions are made every month. Your child therefore needs to be ready when that visit is booked. I advise starting preparation one to two terms beforehand.
