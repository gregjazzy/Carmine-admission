Aiglon College: should you prepare for the entrance assessments?

Villars-sur-Ollon, home of Aiglon. Photo Bledard92, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Boarding schools November 2026 5 min read

On this point Aiglon is unusually frank: there is no need, the school writes, to prepare for its entrance assessments. They are there to see where an applicant's strengths and weaknesses lie before they arrive, and they are only one part of the application: the school looks at the whole student, not just results.

That is true. And it is exactly what traps many families. They conclude there is nothing to do, and arrive at admission without having prepared what, at Aiglon, really decides.

What the assessments measure

The online assessments cover problem solving, mathematical reasoning, logic and literacy; for Year 12 entry, a maths test with a calculator is added. These are not knowledge tests to be crammed for. They measure a way of reasoning, and a level of written English that will, or will not, allow the child to follow the IGCSEs and then the International Baccalaureate. A child who reasons well but reads English with difficulty will mostly reveal their language difficulties.

Preparing what really decides

I do not cram your child for Aiglon's assessments: I measure their real level in English and reasoning, help them close what needs closing, and prepare them for the interview, where the school discovers who they are. My aim: that they come across as they are, at their best.

What can really be prepared

Three things, in my view, make the difference, and none can be crammed the night before. First, written English, which shapes the whole schooling to come and takes months to build. Then the interview: Aiglon looks for a child who embraces its five principles, challenge, respect, responsibility, diversity and service, and who wants the mountains, expeditions and house life. A child who has never thought about what they expect from a boarding school, or what they can bring to it, misses the point. Finally the application: two years of school reports and recommendations from two teachers, which must describe the same child as the one in the interview.

Is your child at ease in written English? Have them assessed: I will tell you whether they are at Aiglon's level, and what they lack.

The "nothing to prepare" trap

When a school says there is nothing to prepare, it is telling the truth about its tests, and protecting applicants from cramming. But admission remains selective, with a waiting list and declines every month. The families who succeed are those who understood that preparation was not about exercises, but about the child: their English, their maturity, their ability to say who they are.
