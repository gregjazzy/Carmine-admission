The Exeter essays and parent statement

Book a conversation

Most of an Exeter file is written by other people: teachers, a principal or counselor, an adult who knows the child outside school, a testing organisation. Three pieces are not. The child writes two essays of 200 to 500 words, the parents write a statement, and all three are submitted in the Exeter applicant portal by January 15. The prompts appear only once the portal is open.

I have students at Exeter and work on these pieces with families every year. They are short, and that is exactly what makes them demanding.

Five hundred words at most

Five hundred words is a page. It is enough for one idea developed properly, and not enough for a life story. The essays that work at this length choose a single subject and go deep into it: a problem that took weeks to solve, a conversation that changed an opinion, an object, a place, a habit. The ones that fail try to cover everything the child has done, and end up saying nothing that the reports and recommendations have not already said.

The lower limit matters too. An essay of 210 words on a prompt that invites reflection suggests a child who had little to say or little time to say it.

The writing sample on the same desk

The SSAT and the ISEE both include a writing sample, and admissions readers look at it very closely: it is written under test conditions, by the child alone, and it is considered the most accurate measure of how the child really writes. The essays are read with that sample in mind.

That sets a simple rule. An essay polished by an adult, or written at a level the child cannot reach alone, does not just fail to help; it raises a doubt about the whole file. The right essay sounds like the writing sample on a very good day.

Essays in the child's own voice

The best essays are the child's, made better by work. I help the child find the subject, think it through and rewrite it draft after draft, until the essay says something only they could have written.

Finding the subject

Children nearly always start with the wrong subject: the biggest achievement, the most impressive trip, the cause that sounds most serious. The better subject is usually smaller and more personal. Exeter teaches around a table where students are expected to think aloud and to change their minds; an essay that shows a child noticing something, questioning it and reaching a view of their own is a preview of that student.

The test of a good subject is whether the child gets more interesting as they talk about it. If the conversation dries up after two sentences, the essay will too.

Has your child ever written about themselves in English? Tell me about them: the first draft should exist by November.

Writing in a language that is not the school's

For a child educated in another language, the essays are a test of English as well as of thought. Committees reading files from around the world do not expect native prose from a thirteen-year-old. They expect clarity and a voice. Simple sentences that say something precise are worth more than ambitious vocabulary used slightly wrong, and a phrase that sounds translated is better rewritten in the child's own plain English.

The parent statement

The parent statement is the one place where the family speaks directly. It is not a second recommendation and not a list of qualities. Its use is to describe the child as only parents can: how they learn, what absorbs them, how they deal with difficulty, what they still find hard, and why this school rather than another.

A statement made only of praise tells the reader nothing, and one that contradicts the teachers' letters damages the family's credibility. Honest detail does the opposite. A parent who writes that their son gives up too quickly on problems that do not interest him, and describes what they have seen change, gives the committee something real to work with.

How I work on the essays and the parent statement

I have students at Phillips Exeter and for seven years have worked with around fifteen families a year aiming for the most selective schools in the United States, England and Switzerland. On the essays, I work with the child from the choice of subject to the final draft, and never write in their place. With the parents, I review the statement so that it is honest, specific and consistent with the rest of the file.

These are the only pieces you control entirely. Write to me before the holidays take the time away.
