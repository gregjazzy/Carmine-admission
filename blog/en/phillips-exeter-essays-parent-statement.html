Phillips Exeter: the essays and the parent statement

In Exeter's file, two pieces are written by the family itself: the child's two essays, 200 to 500 words each, and the parent statement. The prompts are given in the applicant portal, and everything must be submitted by January 15.

What the committee looks for

The rest of the file says how the child works: reports, test, teacher recommendations. The essays say who they are. Exeter looks for students who will think aloud around a table of twelve; it wants to hear a voice, ideas of their own, real curiosity.

Essays that ring true

I work on the essays with your child: finding their subject, their voice, and rewriting until the text sounds like them. I never write them in their place; the committee would see it.

The mistakes that cost

The essay written by an adult

The committee reads the SSAT writing sample very closely, as the most accurate measure of the child's writing. An essay at a completely different level stands out, and it ruins confidence in the whole file.

The list of achievements

An essay that lists prizes and activities repeats the rest of the file. A good essay starts from a moment, a question, a detail, and draws something from it.

Has your child ever written about themselves in English? Tell me about them: that is where we start.

The over-praising parent statement

It serves to present the child as their parents see them, including their difficulties and what they still have to learn. A page of praise tells the committee nothing.
