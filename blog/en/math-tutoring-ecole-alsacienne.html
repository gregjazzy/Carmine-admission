Math Private Lessons for Ecole Alsacienne Students

Private Tutoring February 2026 11 min read

The Ecole Alsacienne occupies a singular position in the Parisian educational landscape. Located on rue d'Assas in the heart of Paris's 6th arrondissement, it is neither a traditional Catholic school nor an elite public institution. It is a private secular school, founded in 1874, that has cultivated for nearly 150 years a distinctive pedagogical approach: intellectual rigor, open-mindedness, and student autonomy. The families who choose the Alsacienne -- intellectuals, artists, diplomats, senior civil servants, business leaders -- do so for this promise: to form thinkers, not executors. But this pedagogical ambition creates a paradox in mathematics. Alsacienne students often understand more deeply than their peers. They sometimes perform below what their actual level should allow. And this is exactly where the intervention of a specialized math tutor makes all the difference.

Having worked with over 1,600 students, including many from the Ecole Alsacienne, I know this school from the inside. I know its strengths, its blind spots, and above all the particular profile of the Alsacienne student who struggles in math. This is not a weak student. It is a brilliant student whose brilliance has not yet translated into results.

The Alsacienne pedagogy: deep understanding, but how to perform?

The Ecole Alsacienne is distinguished by a pedagogy that prizes deep comprehension over mechanical application. Teachers encourage questioning, critical reflection, intellectual debate. In mathematics classes, this approach translates into teaching that values intuition and the construction of reasoning over the repetition of procedures. An Alsacienne student generally knows how to explain why a theorem is true. They can contextualize a concept, connect it to other domains, see the "big picture."

This is a considerable strength. But it has a downside in the context of examinations. The baccalaureat, preparatory class entrance selections, and even internal assessments demand something beyond understanding: they demand performance under time pressure. And performance under time pressure relies on automatisms, execution speed, and formal rigor in mathematical writing that are not always the priorities of the Alsacienne pedagogy. A student who perfectly understands the Mean Value Theorem but takes twenty minutes to write a complete proof underperforms relative to a less brilliant student who is better trained in exam mechanics.

This gap between understanding and performance is the heart of the problem for many Alsacienne students. And it is a problem that a generic math tutor cannot address, because they do not see it. They see a student who "has the level" but "cannot deliver," and they wrongly conclude that the student is not working hard enough. The reality is more nuanced. The student needs specific work on exam technique, speed, and formalization -- without destroying the deep understanding that is their strength.

The math program at the Alsacienne: demanding and ambitious

The mathematics program at the Ecole Alsacienne is ambitious. The school aims for academic excellence, and the mathematics teaching staff is of high caliber. The mathematics specialization classes in Premiere and Terminale are dense, exercises are demanding, and assessments are designed to differentiate students. The pace is sustained, even though the pedagogical approach allows more room for exploration than a more traditional institution.

Post-bac destinations for Alsacienne students reflect this ambition. A significant proportion targets scientific preparatory classes -- MPSI at Louis-le-Grand, Henri IV, Sainte-Genevieve -- while others head toward Sciences Po, ECG preparatory classes for HEC, or top international universities: Ivy League, Oxbridge, EPFL, ETH Zurich. For each of these destinations, the mathematics record is a determining factor. A 13/20 average in math at the Alsacienne can be perceived very differently depending on the target destination. For an elite MPSI prepa, it is insufficient. For Sciences Po, it is acceptable. The private lesson strategy must adapt to the objective, not just the curriculum.

The gap between understanding and succeeding: the real Alsacienne challenge

Let me describe a profile I encounter regularly. A Premiere student at the Alsacienne, in the math specialization. Intelligent, curious, capable of sophisticated mathematical discussions. In class, they understand everything. They ask pertinent questions. Their teacher knows they have the level. And yet, on the written assessment, they score 11/20. The student is frustrated. The parents are puzzled. The teacher says "they have the potential, they just need to work more." But the student is already working. The problem is not the volume of work. It is the nature of the work.

This student spends their study hours re-reading the course, understanding proofs, exploring concepts. This is exactly what the Alsacienne pedagogy has trained them to do. But they do not practice solving exercises under time constraints. They do not train in writing under pressure. They do not develop the automatisms that allow them to handle calculation questions in three minutes instead of seven. And on the assessment, those seven minutes lost on each calculation question become forty minutes lost across the entire paper. They do not have time to finish. Their grade does not reflect their level. The cycle repeats.

A specialized math tutor for the Ecole Alsacienne understands this mechanism. They do not ask the student to abandon their way of thinking. They add a layer of technique on top of the understanding. They train speed without sacrificing depth. It is a delicate balance, and it is exactly what our private lessons at Carmine are designed to achieve.

The social dimension: at the Alsacienne, struggle is hidden

There is a specific aspect of the Ecole Alsacienne that outside tutors do not always suspect. At this school, academic difficulty is taboo. The social milieu of Alsacienne families -- intellectual, cultivated, often affluent -- creates an implicit pressure: one does not discuss poor grades. An Alsacienne student scoring 9/20 in math does not tell their classmates. They do not easily discuss it with their parents either, who expect their child to succeed "naturally" in such a stimulating environment.

This culture of silence compounds the problem. The student accumulates gaps in silence, convinced they are the only one who does not understand, when in reality a significant number of their classmates experience the same thing. Private math lessons, in this context, are not merely academic support. They are a confidential space where the student can acknowledge their difficulties without judgment and work on them without social pressure.

Physics and chemistry: same logic, same stakes

In physics and chemistry, Alsacienne students encounter the same type of disconnect. Qualitative understanding of phenomena is often excellent -- the school's pedagogical approach naturally lends itself to this. But equation setup, the mathematical formalism applied to physics, and solving quantitative problems under time constraints present the same difficulties as in pure mathematics. A student who intuitively understands the principle of energy conservation but cannot correctly set up the equations in thirty seconds loses precious time on assessments.

Our physics and chemistry tutors for the Alsacienne work with the same approach as in math: they preserve the student's deep understanding while adding the technical automatisms necessary for performance. The result is a student who understands and performs -- exactly the profile that the most selective post-bac programs seek.
