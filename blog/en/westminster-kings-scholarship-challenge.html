Westminster King's Scholarships and The Challenge

King's Scholarships are Westminster's academic scholarships, established by Henry VIII and affirmed by Elizabeth I in 1560. Westminster has 48 King's Scholars at any time, usually twelve new ones each year: up to eight at 13+, chosen through an examination called The Challenge, and up to four at 16+, chosen through the Sixth Form entrance process. They all board in College, one of the school's six boarding houses, attend services in Westminster Abbey at the Dean's invitation and carry out ceremonial duties linked to the Abbey and the Crown.

What a King's Scholarship is worth

King's Scholarships are not means-tested. Their value is practical as well as honorary: a King's Scholar boards in College but pays only the day fee, £16,456 a term at 13+ and £17,482 at 16+ for 2026-27, against £23,094 for an ordinary boarder. Where a family can show financial need, the scholarship can be supplemented by a bursary up to the full fee.

The Challenge: who can sit it

The Challenge is sat at Westminster in mid-May of Year 8. Candidates must be under 14 on 1 September of the year of entry. They do not need to be registered for the school or to hold a place already; but a candidate without a place who does not win a King's Scholarship will not be offered an ordinary place on the strength of The Challenge.

The application form and information booklet are available from October of Year 8. The current head must countersign the form, which must be returned by 28 February of Year 8. The first conversation, therefore, is with the head: Westminster expects candidates whose school believes they are of scholarship standard.

The papers, from 2027

The Challenge has been reshaped, with first examination in 2027. Every candidate sits seven core papers of 60 minutes each, except Science at 90 minutes. Candidates who have not studied Latin or French sit a Linguistics and Language paper in their place. Each candidate then chooses two specialisms, no more, and not all are written: a Drama candidate offers two monologues, an artist or scientist may complete a practical, a linguist may take a French oral, a classicist a Greek paper, a humanities enthusiast an interdisciplinary paper across history, geography and philosophy, and strong mathematicians and writers can take supplementary specialism papers in Mathematics and English. Syllabus documents and specimen materials are published for each.

Alongside the King's Scholarships, The Challenge now leads to Head Master's Awards, for overall academic excellence and for strong performance in a particular subject.

Choosing the specialisms

The two specialisms are the candidate's chance to show where they are exceptional. A strong mathematician should almost certainly take the mathematics specialism; a candidate who writes with flair, the English one. The less obvious choices, the interdisciplinary humanities paper, the Greek, a drama or art practical, suit candidates with a genuine and developed interest, not those looking for an easier option. The published syllabus documents should drive the choice.

At 16+

Up to four King's Scholarships are awarded to new Sixth Form pupils on their overall performance in the November entrance exams and interviews. Candidates must be under 17 on 1 September of the year of entry, and indicate their interest on the Sixth Form registration form. As at 13+, King's Scholars board in College.

Music, too

Up to eight Music Awards at 13+, each worth 10 percent of the day fee with free tuition on two instruments, plus three music exhibitions; candidates usually play to a good Grade 7 standard and audition in late January or early February of Year 8. At 16+, up to four Music Scholarships for Grade 8 players. Music award holders without a place must still qualify through The Challenge or Common Entrance.

How I prepare Challenge candidates

I work through the published specimen materials paper by paper, with most weight on mathematics, science and English, and prepare the two specialisms in depth once chosen. Throughout, the aim is the kind of thinking a scholarship examination rewards: taking an unfamiliar question seriously, reasoning on paper, and writing an answer that has a point.
