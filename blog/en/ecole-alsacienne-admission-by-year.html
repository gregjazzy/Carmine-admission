École alsacienne: entry in nursery, CP, CM1, sixième or seconde

The École alsacienne, Paris 6th. Photo Celette, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Many families think of the École alsacienne as a single application: you submit a file, the school says yes or no. In reality, there are five different admissions, one per entry point, each with its own places, tests and traps. Choosing the right door, at the right age, often counts for more than everything else.

I have students at the Alsacienne at every level, and every year I support families applying there. Here, door by door, is what is really at stake.

Five doors, and almost no others

The school only opens real places at five levels: 26 in moyenne section (the middle year of nursery, age 4), 26 in CP (age 6), 28 in CM1 (age 9), around sixty in sixième (age 11) and around thirty in seconde (age 15). In the other primary years, there are no places. In the other collège and lycée years, fewer than ten, which only open when a student leaves. A family aiming for CE2 or quatrième is therefore applying for places that may never exist.

Nursery and CP: the family has the interview

At the first two doors, the school meets the parents. It is looking for families genuinely committed to its project: a secular, caring education that rejects competition. It is also the only point at which brothers and sisters of current students get priority, so some of the places are taken before the selection even begins. For a family with no link to the school, it is a narrower door than it looks, and it turns on the sincerity of the family's commitment, not on the child's abilities.

The right door, at the right time

I help you choose the entry year where your child has the best chance, then prepare their application, test and interview to the standard of a school that keeps one applicant in seven. My aim: that they get into the École alsacienne through the door that suits them.

CM1: the door families forget

CM1 is, in my view, the most underestimated door. It opens 28 places, as many as CP, but with an entirely different selection: a sift of school reports, a French and maths test, then an interview with the child alone and a teacher. Siblings no longer get priority. And a child who enters in CM1 will not have to face the sixième selection, the most contested of all. For a family that discovers the school late, or whose child has matured since CP, it is often the best window.

Sixième: the biggest door, and the most contested

It is the school's largest intake, with around sixty places. But about fifteen are reserved for the music class, which recruits by audition among children enrolled at a conservatoire or music school. For everyone else, the door is closer to forty-five places. The selection follows that of CM1: CM1 and CM2 school reports, a French and maths test in which consistent results are valued, then an interview. It is the CM1 reports that open the file: sixième is prepared two years ahead.

Seconde: around thirty places, and an administrative trap

In seconde there is no longer a written test: selection is based on the troisième and quatrième reports, then the interview. Places are few, against students coming from the best collèges in Paris. And a student at a Paris state collège must also go through the education authority's placement procedure and list the École alsacienne as first choice: an offer is useless if the choices were not listed in the right order.

How I support families, at each door

For seven years, I have supported around fifteen families a year with their admissions to the leading Paris schools. For the Alsacienne, my work starts with choosing the door: from an assessment at the school's level, in French, maths and English, I tell you at which level your child has the best chance. I prepare what that door requires: the parents' interview in nursery and CP, the child's test and interview in CM1 and sixième, the application and interview in seconde. And because only one applicant in seven is accepted, I build with you the list of schools to aim for in parallel.
