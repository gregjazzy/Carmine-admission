UK boarding schools

Eton or Le Rosey

Families who hesitate between Eton and Le Rosey are usually choosing between two ideas of a great boarding school more than between two addresses. One is an English school of about 1,350 boys, founded in 1440, which decides who it will take when they are eleven. The other is a Swiss school of about 450 pupils from 8 to 18, spread over two campuses, which admits all year round and watches the balance of nationalities very closely.

I have students in both schools, and fifteen of my students have been admitted to Le Rosey. The differences that matter for a family are not always the ones in the brochures.

When the decision is made

At Eton, everything runs on one calendar: registration by 31 August of Year 5, the ISEB Pre-Tests in the autumn of Year 6, Stage 2 in the spring, then confirmation by examination in Year 8 for entry at thirteen. A family that arrives late finds most of the doors closed, and the remaining ones (the King's Scholarship, the Sixth Form at sixteen) are narrower.

Le Rosey works on rolling admissions for its younger classes. A candidate spends a day on campus, no earlier than fifteen months before the year he wants to join, sits tests in maths, in English or French, and in his mother tongue, and is interviewed by an academic adviser. A committee meets every week. The school advises applying between June and September of the year before entry, and from the equivalent of Year 10 it works in fixed rounds. It is also openly reluctant to take pupils for only the last two years.

Two schools, one strategy

Eton and Le Rosey do not test the same things, nor at the same age. I prepare candidates for both, and help families build a plan where each application strengthens the other instead of competing with it.

What each school is looking for

Eton's selection is built around standardised measures and an interview: four adaptive tests and a school report at Stage 1, then an individual interview and an online test of potential. The question it asks is whether a boy has the ability and the temperament to thrive among very able boys in a house of fifty-five.

Le Rosey asks whether a pupil can follow the programmes leading to the IB Diploma or the French baccalaureate, in English or French, and whether his family shares the school's values. Talent in sport or the arts is a crucial component of an application there. And a factor Eton does not have weighs heavily: strict quotas by nationality and language group, at every level. An excellent candidate can be deferred or placed on a waiting list simply because his group is full.

Hesitating between the two? Tell me about your child: the right choice depends on his profile, not on the schools' reputations.

What they teach

Eton follows the English route: GCSEs, then A levels, with 24 GCSE and 30 A level subjects on offer and nine modern and classical languages. In 2025, 85% of its GCSE grades were 8 or 9. Le Rosey prepares for the IB Diploma or the French baccalaureate, teaches bilingually, and many of its pupils add two or three further languages. Its year is split between the main campus in Rolle and a winter campus in Gstaad.

The question is less which programme is better than which suits the university plan. A boy aiming firmly at Oxford or Cambridge in a single subject is well served by A levels; a pupil whose plans span several countries often benefits from the breadth of the IB.

Money

At Eton, the 2026/27 fee is £65,673 a year including VAT. At Le Rosey, boarding and tuition for 2027-28 cost CHF 164,400 a year from Class 5, before the annual IB charge of CHF 13,900 in the last two years. The philosophies differ even more than the figures. Eton has a large endowment, spent £10.6 million on financial support in 2024/25 and helps 22% of its boys, some up to full fees. Le Rosey has no endowment, accepts no donations and awards three to five partial scholarships a year, never full ones, and only from Class 3 upwards. Le Rosey never pays agents, and at neither school does money buy a place.

Applying to both

The two calendars can be combined, provided the family starts with the stricter one. Registering for Eton by the end of Year 5 costs £480 and keeps the door open; Le Rosey can then be approached in its own window, with tests that call on different strengths. For a family abroad, the year of entry has to be checked in both systems: Year 9 at Eton corresponds to Class 4 at Le Rosey.
