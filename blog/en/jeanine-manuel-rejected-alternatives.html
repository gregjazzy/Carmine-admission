Rejected by Jeannine Manuel: Alternatives and Rebound Strategy

School Integration February 2026 9 min read

Your child was not admitted to Ecole Jeannine Manuel. The email arrived, and with it, a wave of disappointment, frustration, and possibly anger. You invested months of preparation. You believed the profile was strong. And the school said no.

Before you do anything else, hear this: a rejection from Jeannine Manuel is a verdict on fit, not on your child. It is not a measure of their intelligence, their worth, or their potential. It is a statement that, in this particular admissions cycle, for this particular set of available spots, the school determined that another configuration served its institutional needs better. That is a fact, not a comfort -- but understanding it correctly is the foundation for everything that follows.

Why students get rejected

EJM does not provide detailed feedback on rejections, which leaves families to speculate -- often unproductively. After more than 1,600 students guided through competitive admissions, I can identify the patterns with reasonable precision. Rejections at EJM typically fall into one of several categories.

Timing

The application was submitted too late, at an entry level with very few spots, or in a year with unusually high competition. Timing is not within a family's control once the application is submitted, but it is the single most common factor in rejections of otherwise strong candidates. A qualified student applying for 2nde in a year when only three external spots are available faces odds that no amount of preparation can overcome.

Academic gaps

The entrance tests revealed a gap between the candidate's level and EJM's expectations. The most common gaps are in academic French (particularly for students who have been educated abroad) and in mathematics (where curricular differences between the French system and other systems create blind spots). These gaps are often invisible to the family because the child performs well in their current school -- but EJM's tests are calibrated to the French national curriculum, not to the candidate's home system.

Language asymmetry

EJM is a bilingual school, and it requires genuine bilingualism in both French and English. A candidate who is excellent in English but merely functional in academic French, or who speaks beautiful French at home but cannot write a structured essay, presents a risk the school is unwilling to take. Asymmetric bilingualism is one of the most common rejection triggers, and one that families consistently underestimate.

Profile mismatch

Sometimes the candidate's profile -- personality, interests, temperament -- does not align with what EJM seeks. The school selects for a specific type of student: intellectually curious, adaptable, internationally minded, and socially engaged. A brilliant but introverted student who shows no interest in the international dimension, or a high achiever who lacks the curiosity and flexibility EJM values, may be rejected despite strong test scores. This is not a flaw in the student. It is a mismatch between the student and the institution.

Processing the rejection: what families need to do first

Before strategy, before alternatives, before reapplication planning -- there is an emotional reality that must be addressed. And I say this as someone who has sat with hundreds of families in this exact moment: the emotional processing is not optional. It is prerequisite.

For the parent

Your disappointment is real and valid. You may feel that you failed your child, that you should have prepared differently, that the system is unfair. All of those feelings are natural. But they must not drive your next decisions. The worst strategic moves I have witnessed in fifteen years of admissions consulting have been made by parents acting from disappointment rather than analysis.

Give yourself a week. Do not make any decisions about alternative schools, reapplication, or your child's educational future for at least seven days. The clarity you need will not come from the first wave of emotion.

For the child

Your child is watching you. How you react to this rejection will teach them something fundamental about how to handle setbacks. If you catastrophize, they will learn that a single institutional decision defines their future. If you blame the school, they will learn that external attribution is an acceptable response to disappointment. If you process the rejection with composure, analyze what happened honestly, and move forward constructively, they will learn something far more valuable than anything EJM could have taught them.

Tell your child the truth: the school had more qualified applicants than spots. They were not chosen this time. That does not change who they are or what they are capable of. And then show them, through your actions, that there is a plan.

The alternatives: schools that deserve serious consideration

The landscape of international and bilingual education in the Paris region is broader than many families realize. EJM is exceptional, but it is not the only path to an exceptional education. Here are the institutions that I consistently recommend as alternatives.

Lycee International de Saint-Germain-en-Laye

This is the most direct alternative to EJM in terms of academic rigor and international orientation. Saint-Germain is a public institution with fourteen national sections, offering the BFI through each section. The American and British sections are particularly strong. The school is free (with modest section fees), located in an affluent suburb, and produces outstanding university outcomes. For families willing to relocate to the western suburbs, Saint-Germain is often the superior choice on a cost-adjusted basis. For a detailed comparison, see our analysis of Jeannine Manuel vs Saint-Germain-en-Laye.

Franklin (Lycee International de Paris Honore de Balzac)

Located in the 17th arrondissement, Franklin offers international sections within a public lycee framework. The school is less prestigious than EJM or Saint-Germain but provides a solid bilingual education and access to the BFI. For families who need to stay in Paris and cannot afford private school tuition, Franklin is a pragmatic and respectable option.

EABJM (Ecole Active Bilingue Jeannine Manuel)

Despite the confusingly similar name, EABJM is a distinct institution from EJM. It is a private bilingual school with its own pedagogical approach and admissions process. The school serves a different profile -- typically families who want bilingual education but whose children may not meet EJM's selectivity threshold. It is a legitimate alternative that provides genuine bilingual instruction, though the academic environment and post-bac outcomes differ from EJM's.

IB schools in the Paris region

For families open to the International Baccalaureate rather than the French bac, several institutions merit consideration:

  • International School of Paris (ISP): a well-established IB World School in the 16th arrondissement. Fully anglophone, with strong university placement globally.
  • British School of Paris: follows the British curriculum through GCSEs with an IB Diploma Programme option. Strong for families with UK university ambitions.
  • American School of Paris (ASP): located in Saint-Cloud, offering an American curriculum with IB and AP options. Strong for US university admissions.

The IB pathway sacrifices some of the BFI's integration with the French system but offers broader international recognition and a different pedagogical philosophy that may suit your child better.

Reapplication: when and how

Reapplying to EJM after a rejection is possible and, in many cases, advisable -- but only under specific conditions.

When reapplication makes sense

Reapplication is strategically sound when the rejection was driven by factors that can be addressed within one academic year. If the issue was academic French, a year of intensive French instruction can close the gap. If the issue was mathematics, systematic preparation aligned with the French curriculum can fill the holes. If the issue was timing (a highly competitive year with very few spots), reapplying in a different year may produce a different result.

When reapplication does not make sense

If the rejection was driven by a fundamental profile mismatch -- if your child's personality, interests, and temperament do not align with what EJM seeks -- reapplication is unlikely to produce a different outcome. The school will remember the candidate, and a second application that is substantively identical to the first will be recognized as such. Reapplication only works when there is genuine, demonstrable growth between the first and second application.

The reapplication strategy

A successful reapplication requires three elements:

  1. Honest diagnosis: identify, as precisely as possible, why the first application failed. This requires setting aside ego and analyzing the candidate's profile against EJM's known criteria with clinical objectivity.
  2. Targeted development: spend the intervening year addressing the identified gaps. This is not about general improvement. It is about specific, measurable progress in the areas that caused the rejection. If academic French was the issue, the student should be producing French essays at the level EJM expects by the time the second application is submitted.
  3. A stronger narrative: the second application must tell a story of growth. The school should be able to see, clearly, what has changed. New experiences, new achievements, and evidence of maturity and self-awareness are all valuable additions.

Using rejection constructively

I will share something I have observed across hundreds of families: the students who are rejected by EJM and go on to thrive at alternative schools often develop a resilience, a drive, and a self-awareness that their peers who were admitted directly do not. Rejection, processed correctly, becomes fuel.

A student who is rejected at 12 and reapplies successfully at 14 arrives at EJM with a maturity and determination that the school recognizes and values. A student who is rejected and excels at Saint-Germain or an IB school arrives at university with the same credential and often a more interesting personal story. The path matters less than the student's response to it.

I tell every family I work with the same thing: the school is a lever, not a destination. EJM is an excellent lever. But it is one of several. The student who develops discipline, intellectual curiosity, and the ability to recover from setbacks will succeed regardless of which institution provides the framework. The rejection letter does not close doors. It redirects the path.

What to do starting today

If you have received a rejection from EJM and you are reading this article, here is your immediate action plan:

  1. This week: process the emotional impact. Do not make strategic decisions yet. Talk to your child honestly and calmly.
  2. Next week: activate alternative school applications if you have not already. Saint-Germain, Franklin, and EABJM have their own deadlines, and some may still be accepting applications depending on timing.
  3. Within a month: conduct a thorough post-mortem of the EJM application. What were the likely reasons for rejection? What can be addressed? Is reapplication viable?
  4. Within two months: if reapplication is the decision, begin the development plan. Academic French tutoring, mathematics alignment, interview preparation -- the clock for next year's application starts now.
  5. Ongoing: invest fully in whichever school your child attends. The alternative is not a consolation prize. It is the platform from which your child will build their future. Treat it accordingly.
