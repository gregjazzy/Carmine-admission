Parcoursup coach: what is worth paying for, and when

Parcoursup October 2026 8 min read

Greg MitteletteFondateur de Carmine Admission. 1 100 élèves accompagnés en 14 ans, admis notamment à Stanford, Columbia, Oxford, l'EPFL et Bocconi.

Prendre rendez-vous

Parcoursup is the single national platform through which French lycée students, and anyone else holding or sitting the French baccalauréat, apply to almost every first-year programme in France: universities, the two-year prépa classes that feed the grandes écoles, post-bac engineering and business schools, BTS and BUT vocational degrees. For a family used to UCAS or the Common App, it looks simple on paper and behaves strangely in practice. That gap has created a market for private coaches.

In June 2026, the French higher education minister told the parliamentary channel LCP that he "very strongly" advised families against paying one. His reasoning: there is no strategy to sell, because choices are entered unranked. For January, when students enter their choices, he is right. Each programme sees only its own application and nothing tells it where it stands in the student's list.

Two weeks before that interview, though, the platform had asked every student still on a waiting list to rank those choices within four days. Once a student receives an offer from a ranked choice, every choice ranked below it is deleted. That is the moment a family can lose a place it would otherwise have had, far more than in January.

So the useful question for a parent is what the money should buy, and in which month. Press surveys put the market anywhere from under €100 to over €3,500, and the profession is not regulated; no certification is required. Comparing fees tells you little. Comparing what each offer covers, month by month, tells you a lot.

The part the school controls

Some of the file never passes through the family's hands. Report cards from the last two years of lycée (première and terminale, roughly Year 12 and Year 13) are uploaded by the school, with the student's average, the class average, the highest and lowest marks and each teacher's comments. Absences, despite a persistent rumour, are not included.

Then there is the fiche Avenir. At the second class council of the final year, the teachers review each student's list of choices, and the head of the school gives an opinion on the student's ability to succeed, choice by choice. The school therefore sees the whole list, while each programme sees only its own line. Students can read their fiche Avenir only once the admission phase opens, when nothing can be changed.

The practical consequence is modest but real. The conversation that matters with the form teacher (the professeur principal) happens before that second council. A student who has explained why he wants a science prépa with 13/20 in physics stands a better chance of a reasoned opinion than one whose application lands on the table unexplained. For a student who arrived from abroad midway through lycée and whom teachers barely know, the problem is different, and I cover it in Parcoursup for returning expats.

January to April: building the file

This is the part most coaching offers focus on, yet it is the most tightly framed. Up to ten choices, unranked, plus ten more for apprenticeship programmes. What families miscount is the sub-choices. A prépa is requested by track at national level: "MPSI" counts as one choice and each lycée you ask for counts as a sub-choice, with a cap of ten per choice and twenty in total. BTS, BUT and the DCG accounting degree work the same way. A student who wants eight MPSI classes, six MP2I classes and a couple of computing BUTs as a safety net has used up the quota before the list is finished.

For families aiming at prépas, the real trade-off sits inside those twenty slots: dropping a famous lycée where the file has no statistical chance, to keep a slot in a less known class that admits comparable profiles. Public admission data per class makes that possible, and I have gathered it in Which prépa with which grades.

The motivation statement (still called the projet de formation motivé by most teachers, now simply a "lettre de motivation" on the platform) is required only when the programme asks for it, and is limited to 1,500 characters. Since the 2026 session, each programme states how it uses it: read for every file, used only to separate two close applications, or used to confirm a first assessment. Checking that line before writing saves a weekend spent on a letter the panel will open only in case of a tie. The official Parcoursup presentation goes as far as telling students: "do not write ChatGPT motivation letters!" A letter written by an adult shows just as quickly.

Two sections are often misread. "Autres projets" (other plans) is compulsory but confidential: a student can mention an application to a UK or US university without any French programme knowing. "Activités et centres d'intérêt" is optional and is the only place to mention a competition, a commitment or an internship. Three well-chosen sentences there do more than a dry line.

Interviews, where practice shows

Some selective programmes go beyond the file and call candidates to written or oral tests. They say so on their programme page, and parents who have entered their email and mobile number in their child's account receive the deadline reminders. A post-bac admission interview almost always tests the fit between what the student wrote, what he has done and the programme he is asking for. A bright seventeen-year-old who meets "why us rather than elsewhere?" for the first time in front of the panel loses his footing more often than parents expect. Two or three mock interviews with someone who follows up the way a panel would make a visible difference. For a student who writes well but says little, I think those are the best-spent hours of the year.

June: the week a mistake costs a place

Offers arrive from the first day of the admission phase. In 2025, according to the figures Parcoursup published, 67.9% of lycée students had an offer on day one, and each received 5.6 offers on average. Every offer needs an answer within a short deadline, usually two days, or it passes to the next candidate. Deadlines are paused during the written bac exams.

Then comes the compulsory ranking of waiting-list choices, over four days. Anything left unranked disappears. Anything ranked works like a ladder: as soon as an offer arrives on one choice, every choice ranked below it is deleted so that waiting lists move faster. Programmes never see the ranking, and it does not change the student's position on their lists.

The most likely mistake goes like this. The student ranks first the choice where he is "well placed", out of caution, and his favourite prépa third. The first offer comes through and the third choice is wiped out, just as it was climbing. The platform's own guidance says no strategy is needed, which is true, since no programme sees the order; that is the reason to rank by genuine preference, the place he would actually go first, with no attempt to weigh the odds. For a teenager who has hesitated since January, the question arrives brutally that week, and it is better settled in May, calmly, with his parents.

The ranking has a quiet afterlife. At the end of the main phase, choices still on a waiting list are archived in the order chosen, and a place freed over the summer by a withdrawal is offered to the first candidate on that archived list. A family leaving on holiday in early July should know it.

Example: the 2027 intake, from the calendar published on parcoursup.gouv.fr.

17 December 2026: programme catalogue goes live. 18 January to 11 March 2027: registration and entry of choices. 1 April 2027: last day to complete the file and confirm choices. 1 June 2027: main admission phase begins. 4 to 7 June 2027 inclusive: ranking of waiting-list choices. 10 June 2027: supplementary phase opens, with up to ten new choices. 10 July 2027: main phase ends. 9 September 2027: supplementary phase ends.

Telling a useful adviser from the rest

Families who have described their experience to the French education press say much the same things. A mother whose son was finishing at a French lycée abroad had hired a coach because they lacked the information families in France pick up naturally; she was satisfied, but admitted the coach had drawn up all ten choices herself and that her son had leaned on her. A student admitted to the law degree she wanted, after spending €850 on coaching, felt her school had given her as much. In the first case the coach did the student's work; in the second, she repeated the school's.

I would check three things. Does the person read the selection criteria and admission figures published on each programme page, or talk about "strategy" in general? Does she make the student write, or write for him? And will she be reachable between 1 and 10 June, when offers come in with two days to answer? Support that stops on 1 April leaves the family alone at the most delicate point.

It also depends on the student. One applying to an open-access university degree near home needs nobody. One competing for a prépa, a double degree or a selective post-bac school with a 15/20 average is in the zone where details decide, and that work starts well before the final year, as I explain in When to start preparing university admissions.
