The London 11+ Consortium assessment

Godolphin and Latymer School, Hammersmith. Photo Des Blenkinsopp, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0

London schools November 2026 6 min read

The first stage of entry to Godolphin at 11+ is not Godolphin's own: it is the assessment shared by the thirteen girls' schools of the London 11+ Consortium. It was redesigned for entry in September 2023 with a test provider, Quest Assessments, to measure not only verbal and mathematical ability but creative and critical thinking. Understanding how it is built is the best preparation a family can make.

Once a section is finished, a candidate cannot go back to it. A clock on screen counts down in minutes, with no further warnings.

What each section measures

  • Maths is based on the Year 5 National Curriculum, nothing from Year 6, with challenge coming from the style and depth of the questions.
  • English is a piece of fiction written for the test: comprehension, then recognising correct standard English in context. Inferring the meaning of unknown words is part of what is assessed.
  • Verbal and non-verbal reasoning test vocabulary, patterns and logic.
  • Problem solving asks for multi-step problems using words and numbers.
  • Creative comprehension requires combining information from several sources and formats, in an unfamiliar context.

Depth, not drilling

I work with girls on the mathematics and reading that the Consortium test rewards: Year 5 content explored in depth, multi-step problems, and texts read closely.

Where and when

For entry in September 2027, the assessment runs on 27 November, 1 December or 3 December 2026; Godolphin lists the first two for its own candidates. A girl sits it at her current school if the school can host it, otherwise at a Consortium school she has applied to. She takes it only once, and the result is shared with every Consortium school on her list. Girls abroad can travel to a Consortium school or ask about sitting overseas.

How much preparation?

The Consortium recommends that every candidate looks at its familiarisation materials, especially for verbal and non-verbal reasoning, whose questions can seem strange at first. Beyond that, it advises against intensive practice, whose gains quickly become marginal. Its message is that the skills tested are those a strong pupil builds in school and through wide reading.

That shapes useful preparation: real mathematical understanding of Year 5 material and beyond, the habit of reading fiction closely, and calm familiarity with the format, rather than hundreds of practice papers.
